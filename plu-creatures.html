Chimeras

Maera is a chimera. She has pale fur on her forearms, two horns that curve at different angles, and the good sense to duck before a branch has the opportunity to settle an argument with one of them. If you met her on the road with Breni, you might also remember the pack she carried. It clinked every time she stepped over a rut. Someone can describe a chimera’s appearance in great detail and still leave you wondering what she was doing with all those tools. I’d like to get to the tools before we’re done.

First, though, we ought to settle what I mean by chimera. You’ll hear the word used for anything that looks as though its parts ought to have come from different creatures. I understand the temptation. Give somebody a set of horns they can name and a coat of fur they can’t, and before long they’ll have decided that the horns came from one animal and the fur came from another. By the time they get to the hands, they generally have a third animal in mind. They can make quite a respectable parade out of a person who was only trying to get past them on the road. When I tell you Maera is a chimera, I mean that’s what she is. The name tells you how to begin looking at her. The rest takes a little attention. A horn may remind you of an animal you know without being the same shape, growing the same way, or doing the same job. Fur may feel familiar under your hand and still lie in a different direction or grow thicker in a different season. You can compare those things. I do it myself. Just remember that when you’re finished comparing them, you still have to explain how they work together on the person in front of you. Maera gives us a good place to begin, but she is not a measuring stick for every chimera. You have met her horns, her fur, and the way she carries a pack. Another chimera may give you quite different things to notice. I would rather you asked what a particular person can do than decided what she ought to be able to do because you have seen Maera do it. Selena says this is a rule I should apply to people who offer to help me move furniture. I shall consider it.

“Are you going to explain that part?” Selena asks.

“I am. I was enjoying how nicely you were looking at me while I said it.”

“I was waiting to see whether you would get there.”

“You can do both, darling.”

She can, too. She’s doing it now.

How a chimera comes to be

When people ask who made a chimera, they often expect a name followed by a list of ingredients. I can explain the work better if I start with something you can feel for yourself. Set your hand flat on the table and press down. Keep pressing while you turn your elbow a little.

You’ll feel the effort move from your wrist up into your arm and shoulder. Change the position of your feet and you’ll feel it travel farther. Your hand stays in almost exactly the same place, while the rest of you does quite a lot to keep it there.

That is the part of making a body that seldom gets written down. Everybody remembers what shape the hand was when you finished. I want to know whether the wrist can turn it without pinching, whether the shoulder can take the weight behind it, and whether the person can reach a high shelf without having to rearrange the furniture first. People have to live in the bodies we give them. The shelf will come up more often than you think.

To understand the beginning of chimeras, think of a body capable of carrying features that put different demands on it. Horns add weight above the neck. Broad forearms can put more force behind the hands, provided the shoulders and back can bear it. Fur changes what the skin can feel and how readily heat leaves the body. Each of those things has consequences. The work is in giving them a way to grow and move together, so the person can spend her attention on what she wants to do instead of having to negotiate with her own balance every time she takes a step. I’m describing the design in broad terms because that much applies whether a chimera has Maera’s particular build or looks quite different from her. A young chimera grows into her own proportions. Her reach changes as her arms lengthen. The space her horns take up changes as they grow. She learns those distances as she goes, much as you learned how far you could lean over a washbasin without putting your nose in it. If nobody has mentioned the noise you made the first time, your family has been unusually kind.

“Do you remember the first person who asked you whether you could make their horns match?”

Selena says.

“I remember what you told me.”

“What did I tell you?”

“You said I should wait until they’d finished telling me what they wanted.”

Selena has saved me a good deal of work that way. People sometimes want a strap that fits. I am inclined to hear the first half of the problem and offer them a completely new shoulder.

What the horns change

Maera’s horns curve in different directions. That means the space she needs on her left may be different from the space she needs on her right, particularly when she turns. You can see her make room for them when she ducks beneath a branch. There’s a good reason to do it early. You can see the branch in front of your eyes. The tips of your horns are above and to the sides of that view. By the time the branch is alongside your face, you should already have decided whether you can pass beneath it.

A turn matters as much as the height of the opening. Imagine a door that’s perfectly tall enough when you walk straight through it. Now imagine somebody calls your name just as you cross the threshold. If you turn your head before your shoulders are clear, the far horn can sweep towards the frame. The doorway hasn’t changed. You’ve changed how much of it you need. That’s one

reason a chimera who knows a building may walk through it without giving the doors a glance, then slow down in a perfectly ordinary house she’s visiting for the first time.

You can make a room easier to use with a few sensible choices. Leave space beside a door where a visitor can turn to take off a coat. Hang your lamp where it won’t catch a horn when somebody straightens from the bench. If you’re offering a seat under a low shelf, consider whether the person will be able to get up from it while holding a full cup. You can learn most of this by asking your guest where she’d like to sit and taking her answer seriously. I have watched people move an entire table when there was a perfectly good chair at the other end of it.

“You moved the table,” Selena says.

“Yes. You were sitting at the other end.”

“There were reasons I chose that end.”

“I hoped to become one of them.”

She’s moved her cup closer to mine. I can continue.

The weight of the horns also matters when a person works. If you spend an afternoon looking down at a low bench, the muscles behind your neck are already doing more than they do when you’re upright. Put weight farther forward and they have farther to pull against. A bench at a comfortable height can make a substantial difference by the end of the day. So can a lamp angled to let someone see the work without leaning in. Neither adjustment requires the customer to know what kind of horns the worker has. It requires looking at where she holds her head while she’s doing the job.

Strength you can use

The best example I have for you comes from a scaffold. Maera held the east scaffold upright while Breni got people off it. That is a good description of what she did. To see how much it tells us, picture the structure while the people are still on it. Every step changes where their weight comes down. Somebody reaches for a rail and pulls on it. Somebody else stops halfway to the ladder because the person ahead has stopped. The load keeps changing, and the person below has to keep the scaffold steady through all of it.

You do that with your legs and back as much as with your arms. Your feet give you something to push against. Your knees and hips let you change the height and direction of that push. Your shoulder gives you a broad place to take the load, and your hands can feel the smaller shifts before they turn into larger ones. How much strength you have matters. Knowing where to put it matters every second you’re under there.

A broad forearm is useful when you can bring it against a surface and spread the pressure. Try holding a heavy basket by a narrow handle, then cradle the same basket against your forearm. The basket weighs the same. The handle makes a small area of your hand do most of the work, while the second position lets you take more of the weight against the arm and bring it nearer your body. Either grip might be right, depending on where the basket is going. If you’ve got to

get through a narrow gate, you may want the handle. If you’ve got to stand there while your wife decides which gate she meant, I recommend the arm.

“I knew which gate I meant,” Selena says.

“So did I, once you came back through it.”

A scaffold is a more serious business than a basket, of course. Maera’s strength bought Breni time. Breni used that time to get people clear. That’s the use I want you to understand. It’s easy to describe strength as the amount a body can lift. What mattered to everyone on those boards was how long Maera could keep the structure steady while they moved. Those are different things to train for. A person can be very good at a short, hard lift and have little practice holding a difficult position while somebody else works at her elbow.

I could make a little scaffold here and show you where the pressure goes.

“Use the pencil,” Selena says.

“I’ve got room.”

“You always have room once you’ve moved the wall.”

I shall use the pencil. If you’re drawing along, draw one upright and put a sloping line against it. That slope is a brace. It sends some of the sideways force down into the ground. Move its foot onto something slick and you can see where the trouble starts. The upright can still look exactly as it did a moment ago. The brace can be just as strong. Once the foot begins to slide, the whole arrangement behaves differently. A person trying to hold it has to account for that change before the people above her have time to understand what they felt.

A coat for the work

Fur deserves a few lines of its own. It helps hold warmth close to the skin, particularly when the coat is dry and traps a layer of air. Rain changes that. So does packing the coat down under a tight strap. If you’ve ever put a wet sleeve back on because you thought it would be easier than carrying it, you already know what a layer can feel like when it stops doing the thing you wanted it for.

Look at where Maera’s pack sits when she carries it. A load that stays close to the back is easier to manage over uneven ground because it has less room to swing away from her. The strap still needs to lie comfortably where it crosses her shoulder. A strap can rub against the direction the fur grows, especially if the bag shifts on every step. A small change in the fastening can take care of it. When somebody asks for a longer strap or a wider pad, give her the measurements she asked for. There’s every chance she’s already walked farther with the old one than you’ll walk with the new one.

The fur on her forearms is also worth remembering if you’re working beside her at a forge. Sparks settle where they land, and loose hair can catch what bare skin would feel quickly enough to shake off. A well-fitted sleeve or guard is a sensible piece of equipment. It should leave the wrist free enough to turn a tool. If it shifts down into the hand every time she reaches, she’ll

spend the whole afternoon pushing it back. You may be surprised how often a perfectly good protective garment ends up on a peg for exactly that reason.

“Is that why you never wear the gloves I gave you?” Selena asks.

“I wear them when you ask me to.”

“I’m asking you now.”

“Then I’ll have to finish the chapter with my gloves on.”

She says I can finish the chapter with the pencil. I’m beginning to suspect she chose the pencil very carefully.

Where you’ll find them

I’ve used a bridge crew to explain chimeras because it gives you a place to see how these features work together. There’s a muddy path, a load to carry, a branch low enough to matter, and timber that will only go into place if several people do their jobs in the right order. Those are useful conditions for understanding almost anyone. They’re also a good deal more ordinary than the circumstances in which people expect to meet a chimera.

Maera was on her way to an old forge with Breni and Sable. Breni needed a new auger because the one she had was twisted. An auger is the tool that makes a hole through the timber, and the one they needed had to reach through the main beams. If it falls short, you have to work from the other side and make the holes meet, which is harder when the wood is thick and the bit wants to follow the grain. If the shaft itself is bent, you can spend a great deal of energy turning it and still end up with a hole in a place you never intended.

Sable had seen smoke upriver. From the air, she could spot a sign that the forge might be working. Maera could help carry what they found back over ground that was already muddy. Breni knew what she needed the tool to do once they got it to the bridge. You can describe that as a harpy, a chimera, and a vampire travelling together. If you want to know why the journey was worth making, you also need to know that one could spot a forge from above the trees, one could take the load on the way back, and one could look at a damaged auger and tell you exactly why it had to be replaced.

That’s the sort of answer I mean to give you when I talk about a people’s place in the world. A chimera can make a home where the work suits her, where her friends are, or where she likes the light through her windows in the morning. For Maera, the place we know is the road between the ravine and the forge, with the bridge going up between them. I can tell you something solid about her there. When a wedge worked loose beneath one of the beams, she saw what had happened and put her shoulder under the timber. Breni drove the wedge back in. The two of them could get on with the job because Maera saw the same problem Breni did and knew which part she could take.

If you meet Maera when she’s carrying her pack, you can still admire the horns. I do. You can even ask her about them, if she has a moment. If she’s on her way to the bridge, ask whether she wants a hand with the pack before you ask how heavy it is. If she gives it to you, I suggest taking

it with both hands. I’ve heard it from the road. Whatever is in there, she has packed rather a lot of it.

“Have you finished?” Selena asks.

“I have. You can let go of my wrist now.”

“I let go when you started drawing.”

Well, that explains the scaffold on the back of the page. If you turn it over, you can see exactly where I put the brace.

Cyclopes

A Cyclops has one eye. If that was the only question you brought to this chapter, I admire your efficiency, and you may go. If your second question is whether I could give a Cyclops another eye, I could also give you a second mouth, but I’d like to see how you get on with the one you have first.

“Plu,” Selena says.

“I said I’d like to see. My hands are flat on the table.”

“One of them is under the table.”

“It’s holding your knee.”

“Oh. Carry on.”

The eye sits in the centre of the face. It moves in its socket, the eyelid closes over it, and it tells a Cyclops most of what you expect an eye to tell anybody. A Cyclops can see you coming. A Cyclops can see the basket of rolls you’ve brought. A Cyclops can see you take one of the rolls on the way over and put the cloth back as though the basket had eaten it. How much of that she chooses to mention will depend on the Cyclops and how many rolls you’ve left her.

There are some differences worth understanding, especially if you work with a Cyclops or expect to compete against one. I’ll get to the contest Selena heard about, although I think she’d prefer that I explain the eye before I explain why a Giant and two teams can disagree about the whereabouts of a line that was supposed to be on the ground.

“I would prefer that,” she says. “I’ve already heard quite enough about the line.”

I suspect everyone has, except the person who moved it. I should very much like to hear that person’s account.

How one eye works

If you have two eyes, hold a finger in front of your face and look at it with one eye closed. Then swap eyes. Your finger seems to jump against whatever’s behind it. You didn’t move it. The two eyes are simply looking from slightly different places. When they’re both open, your mind uses that small difference to help judge how close your finger is.

A Cyclops has other ways of getting that information. Keep one eye closed and move your head a little to the side. Your finger will slide farther across your view than the wall behind it does. The nearer thing seems to move more. With practice, that difference tells you quite a lot. A Cyclops has been practising since she was old enough to reach for something and old enough to

be cross when she missed it. If you’re trying it for the first time because I told you to, I hope you’ll give her credit for the head start.

Motion is only one clue. You know a cup is in front of a plate when it hides part of the plate. You have a fair idea how far down the road a cart is if you know how big the cart ought to look. Watch a hammer come towards you and it gets larger in your view as it approaches. You can use that information to judge when to get out of its way. I would use it quite early myself. There is very little to be gained from precision at that stage of an argument.

A Cyclops may tip her head to get another view before she reaches for something unfamiliar. In a busy workshop, she may set her tools in places she can find with her fingers. If that sounds like a system devised by a creature with one eye, consider how much of your own morning you spend looking for the thing you put down yesterday. I once watched Selena find a needle in the dark without getting out of bed.

“I’d left it in the sewing dish,” Selena says.

“Which you found in the dark.”

“Because I always put it there.”

“Yes, my love. Your gifts are many, and I am making a serious effort to praise the one you’ll let me discuss in public.”

The point is that an eye works with everything else a body knows. Reach gets easier when you know where you left the cup. A familiar set of stairs takes less thought than a strange one. If a Cyclops asks you to hold a board still while she marks it, hold it still. If you move it closer to help, you’ve changed the distance she just judged, and now both of you have to stand there while she decides how much she likes you.

Why the eye is where it is

People sometimes assume a Cyclops started out with the sort of face they’re used to seeing and had an eye taken away. It’s an understandable mistake if you begin with your own face and try to imagine how you’d get to hers. You’ll do better if you begin with a Cyclops. Her face develops around that central eye. The room behind it, the muscles that turn it, and the brow that helps protect it all grow in relation to where it sits. The eye has a place to go from the beginning. As for why the first Cyclopes came into the world with that arrangement, I’ve heard several answers delivered with tremendous confidence. One claims they came from Giants. Another makes their eye the result of an old quarrel between gods. I’ve also heard that I left the second eye off because Selena called me to supper. That last one has the advantage of sounding like something I might do, which is a dreadful standard for deciding what happened.

I have told you three stories people repeat, not three competing records I have checked. The fact that one of them makes me sound hungry is no reason to promote it to history. What I can tell you without leaning on a story is what a Cyclops looks like and the ways she uses that eye. If you want an account of her first beginning, it deserves more care than a rumour told beside a forge.

“You’d have brought the work to supper,” Selena says.

“I did that once.”

“You did it with weather.”

“It was a small front.”

“It rained in the butter.”

I can give you a firmer answer about what the eye does than I can about the first beginning of the Cyclopes. Their living bodies tell me a great deal. A story about a quarrel tells me a great deal about the person who liked it enough to pass it on. I leave a little space between those two kinds of knowledge, even when the story flatters me. Especially when it flatters me in a way that makes Selena laugh into her drink.

There’s a practical consequence to carrying all your sight in one eye. If grit gets in it, there’s no second eye to keep the rest of the room in view while it waters. If you’re shown a visor made for a Cyclops, look at how it protects the whole opening around the eye, particularly where a spark or a chip could come up from below. A guard designed for a person with two eyes can leave a gap in exactly the wrong place for somebody with one. Changing the shape of the protection is far simpler than asking everybody in the forge to persuade the sparks to go around.

I can persuade sparks to go around, incidentally. I can persuade them to spell things, too.

“Leave the sparks alone,” Selena says.

“I was only going to spell your name.”

“You already know it.”

“I like seeing it lit up.”

“I know. I was there for the wedding.”

Mountain forges

Some Cyclopes make their homes in mountain country, and the smiths among them are the ones travellers tend to hear about. A blade travels farther than its maker. By the time it reaches a town several valleys away, everyone knows it came from a Cyclops and almost nobody remembers which Cyclops. Ask who sharpened it last and you’ll get a name, an address, and a running account of what the sharpening cost. People have a wonderful memory for the part of a blade’s history that happened to their purse.

If you want to understand why people make the trip to a mountain forge, look at the ordinary tools on the wall before you look for a sword. A hinge needs to open smoothly after it’s been out in the rain. A wedge has to take repeated blows without breaking where the smith thinned it. The hook holding a pot over a fire needs to fit the chain it hangs from. The work takes patience, and you’re relying on it every time you open a gate, split a log, or sit down expecting to be fed.

Watch a smith checking whether a bar is straight. She can look along it, turn it so the light runs down the edge, and set it against something she knows is flat. If there’s a high spot, the bar may rock under her fingers. She can find that with one eye or two. She may even find it with her eyes shut. The useful thing is knowing which part of the job calls for looking, which calls for feeling, and when to set the piece down and let it cool before you do either.

“Have you ever let anything cool before touching it?” Selena asks.

“Certainly. I let you finish your bath once.”

“You asked whether I’d finished. That was different.”

“I was young.”

“You asked this morning.”

I make a great many mistakes over a very long life, and Selena has the good grace to remember them in the order most convenient to her.

People sometimes say a Cyclops can tell the quality of steel by looking at it. A smith can pick up clues that most of us would miss, and she’ll get others from the sound under the hammer and the way the piece moves when she strikes it. She also knows what she started with and what she’s already done to it. Put a piece of steel in front of her with no history and ask for a judgement, and she may have to test it. That is part of knowing her trade. If all you wanted was somebody who could give you an answer without looking, you could have stayed in town and asked whoever sold you the steel.

There’s a custom I rather like among smiths who work on steep roads. Bring in the old fitting if you want a new one made. A worn hinge tells the person replacing it which side of the gate was dragging. A battered tool shows where it has been struck and whether it was doing a job it could reasonably have been expected to do. You can learn something from a broken thing while it’s still broken. I know this because Selena once kept a latch I’d repaired three times.

“You put it on upside down,” she says.

“I made it work upside down.”

“Three times.”

We bought a new latch. It worked immediately, which I thought showed very little ambition.

The contest

Breni mentioned a contest between the Minotaurs and the Cyclopes. A Giant became involved. By the time Selena heard about it, there were three versions in circulation. One was about strength, one was about the rules, and one involved the possibility that somebody had moved a boundary after losing. If you’ve ever helped run a contest, you’ll understand how all three accounts could have been supplied by the same person before lunch.

Before I go any farther, picture the arrangement: the Minotaurs and Cyclopes were the teams people came to watch, and a Giant became part of the business before anyone had agreed on

which account of the boundary to believe. That is the context for the argument. I have not established which version of the line was correct, and I am not going to award victory from the comfort of Selena’s table. She has informed me that this is the first sensible ruling I have offered all morning.

“Do you know which version was right?” Selena asks.

“I know which one I’d have liked to see.”

“The one where someone moved the boundary.”

“I’d have liked to see how far they got.”

The contest is a useful place to talk about strength because the person doing the lifting is only part of what you’re looking at. Give two people the same weight and one may be able to hold it nearer her chest. Ask them to carry it and the length of their stride begins to matter. Give them a slippery handle and their grip becomes part of the test. A Cyclops with a good grip may hold her own against someone who can lift considerably more in another position. If you want to find out who can move a particular weight a particular distance, lay out that test. If you want to find out who’s strongest in every possible sense, put the kettle on. You’re going to be there a while. An arena also tells you something about how people learn to use their bodies. A Cyclops watching a moving opponent has her whole bearing to read. The way a shoulder comes forward can tell her something before the hand gets there. A foot that turns out may be getting ready to take weight. She’ll make use of distance and timing, as any experienced competitor does. If you spend the match looking into her eye to see whether she’s worked out where you are, she may find that especially helpful. You’ve told her where you’re looking and given her no reason to suppose you’re looking anywhere else.

I’m rather fond of contests with several peoples in them. Somebody always brings a skill that forces the rest of the competitors to think again about what they’re doing. A Giant may move a load in fewer steps. A Minotaur may keep going at a pace somebody else can manage only for the first stretch. A Cyclops who’s worked with timber might take one look at an awkward beam and know where it wants to roll. She may still lose. On Monday morning, when you need that same beam set across a stream, you’ll be glad to know who saw the problem first.

There’s a reason I keep coming back to the work people do after the contest is over. If all the accounts you have of a people were written by someone who came to watch them fight or lift things, you could easily suppose they spent the rest of their lives waiting for another crowd. The smiths have orders to fill. The people who put up the stands may have roofs of their own to mend. Somebody has to take the stakes out of the ground and find out how many mugs were left under the benches. I have considerable respect for whoever does that last job, especially after a Giant has brought a mug.

What they make possible

A good way to see what a mountain forge does for the rest of the world is to follow a tool downhill. It may go to a village that needs a new pin for a mill wheel. It may go to someone

fitting a gate on a steep track where a careless hinge would let the gate swing out and hit the next person coming up. A farmer might bring home an edge she can sharpen herself all season. When the work is good, these things go on doing what they’re supposed to do long after anybody remembers who made the trip to buy them. The mill turns. The gate shuts. Supper arrives at a perfectly reasonable hour. It’s a lovely way to be important, although it does mean people are more likely to think of you when something breaks.

The next time you visit a Cyclops smith, tell her what you need the thing to do. If it’s a hook, tell her what hangs from it. If it’s a latch, tell her which way the door opens and whether it swells in wet weather. She may want to know what you intend to spend. Give her a number before she has to watch your expression change. If she makes a different suggestion, hear her out. She’s the one who has to build the thing, and you’re the one who’ll have to live with what you asked her to make.

The advice applies to the rest of us who make things, I suppose.

“It certainly does,” Selena says.

“What would you like me to make you?”

“Tea.”

“I could make you a cup that keeps it warm for the whole afternoon.”

“Tea in the cup I already have, please.”

She’s quite right. I know what the cup feels like in her hands, how much space she leaves at the top for milk, and which side she turns towards herself because the rim has a chip. I could make a beautiful new cup. I could put her name on it in letters of fire. By then she’ll have got up and put the kettle on herself.

The Cyclops who asks you how your gate opens is saving you exactly that sort of trouble. There are people who make better hinges than I do, and I’m delighted to live in a world where I can buy one. I’d say the same thing about the tea, except that Selena has just put her cup on my side of the table and looked at me in a way I find very difficult to misinterpret.

I’ve got two eyes and a wife who knows how to use both of them. I’ll see you in the next chapter.

Dark Elves

A Dark Elf once told me that if I wanted to know what her people were like, I should come round after supper and listen. I did. By midnight I knew who had made the cushions, why the good mushrooms had been salted twice, and which musician was never again to be allowed to choose the first song. The woman who’d invited me said I was beginning to get the idea. I asked her which idea. She said I’d know once I stopped asking her to explain everybody at once. That was impertinent, and I liked her immediately.

You can learn a few things by looking, which is fortunate, because I like to look. Dark Elves have pointed ears and a range of brown skin tones. In the light of a low fire, you might notice warm copper across one person’s cheekbones and a much cooler brown in the face beside hers. Lift the lamp and look again. You’ll see details the fire flattened, including the expression of the person who’s wondering why you’ve brought a lamp so close to her face. Offer to buy her a drink at that point. It’s what I would do, although I generally think of the drink first. The ear comes to a point above the curve you’d recognise on a human ear. You can see the shape most clearly from the side, where the upper rim draws back and narrows. Some Dark Elves wear their hair tucked behind it, some let it fall across the point, and some put a little piece of metal exactly where you’re likely to notice when they turn their head. If the metal catches your attention, that may have been the intention. If the person catches your attention, I hope you have the manners to tell her yourself.

Now you know where to look for the ears. If you came to find out what “dark” means, you’ll need to stay a little longer.

What the name tells you

Dark Elves are elven people. I can tell you what I know about the early making of elves, and I’ll give those beginnings their own chapter. This name belongs to a history that came afterwards. A people can share an ancestry and still develop ways of living that deserve an account of their own. You can see that in the buildings they choose to keep, in the obligations they recognise, and in what their children learn so early that no one remembers sitting them down to teach it. That matters when people hear the word “Dark” and start supplying meanings of their own. A word about pleasure

Everlangyrs

The Dark Elves and the Goblins had a dispute at Everlangyrs. What to do with what you know

If you want a description you can carry away, remember the ear tapering above the cheek, the change in a face when the light is moved, and the way somebody turns her cup so the handle is where her guest can reach it. have lovely manners. Some will let you finish an entire explanation and then tell you they understood in the first sentence. Occasionally you’ll find both qualities in the same woman, and if you do, I hope you appreciate the trouble she took to let you finish.

What we gave them

When Selena and I made the first dragons, we gave them bodies that could take to the air. There’s a fair amount to that sentence. A dragon has to carry her chest, her tail, the weight of her own wings, and everything she ate for breakfast. Once she’s up, she has to do something about the wind. I had rather a high opinion of the wind when we began making things. I still do, although it has since shown me several sides of its character I wouldn’t have included in a recommendation.

Look at an open wing from below. Close to the body, where the weight has to be carried, you’ll see a strong shoulder and a thick upper reach. Farther out, the wing spreads and the long supports separate. You can see the surface between them draw taut when the dragon opens it fully. Watch the tips when she turns. A tip can flex a little while the rest of the wing holds its shape. Those small changes let her adjust to the air without having to make a grand gesture with her entire body every time the wind changes its mind.

A tail helps with balance. That becomes easier to see when a dragon is coming down towards a ledge. Her head is already looking where she means to put her feet. Her wings are doing much of the slowing. The tail can shift behind her as she brings the weight of her body into line with the landing. Here’s what a rising current offers. When the sun warms a stretch of rock, the air near it can warm and begin to rise. Then one went where I couldn’t see her and stayed there longer than I’d expected. I spent the intervening time discovering how inconvenient it is when the thing you wanted works beautifully. She came back when she was hungry. If you’ve ever waited at a window for somebody you love and then pretended you were there to check the weather, you know how the rest of that afternoon went.

How dragons have changed

If the dragons you know all look alike, I suspect you’ve been meeting them at the same station. That makes sense. A route with heavy loads and long distances will bring you into contact with dragons who can manage heavy loads and long distances. Go somewhere the work is different and give yourself time to look around.

At the ridge I saw a freight dragon with broad wings and a wicker frame hung with bundles. When she drew up her hind legs, they fitted into grooves along her sides. That kept her feet clear

of the load. Another dragon came up the steps below me. Her wings were so narrow and folded so close to her sides that for a moment I thought she was wearing a long coat. That was Istra. She had a rake, a drain to clear, and a pair of foreclaws I would have been very pleased to see on the first dragons.

The claws were long and jointed, with small pads that let her grip a stone by its sharpest edge. She lifted it out of the channel as easily as you might pick a pit out of a bowl of cherries. The first dragons I knew used the tips of their teeth for little things. They were quite good at it. They were also quite good at looking straight past me when they dropped something and heard me laugh. If you’ve ever seen somebody try to pick up a single pea with a pair of serving tongs, you can picture the difficulty. Give the person a pair of tongs she has to wear on her face, and you can picture why she might prefer you to mind your own supper.

Those differences tell you something about how far dragons have come since the first nests. A body can change over many generations. A skill can change in the time it takes one dragon to show another where to put a claw. By the time you see somebody like Istra at work, you’re seeing both. Her foreclaws let her take a delicate hold. Knowing which stones belong in the drain wall and which ones have fallen into the channel is something she had to learn. I can admire what grew and what she learnt with equal pleasure. I’ve had practice. I’ve been admiring dragons for longer than some of the mountains have had their present names.

Istra’s mother carried freight. Istra flew when she was young and could get off the ground readily enough, but her wings gave her less lift than her mother’s. For a while she tried jumping from higher places. I understand the reasoning. If you’re convinced what you lack is nerve, standing somewhere high enough to frighten yourself can feel very much like progress. Then somebody paid her to repair a stair tread, and she went home more pleased with her day than she had ever been after work.

She inspects the steps every fourth day. Her town has routes along the ridge, routes through the air, and routes that pass under the mountain. The steps are a way to reach the workshops, the market, and the warm hall where eggs hatch. Clear a drain and water goes down the channel. Let a loose tile sit in the wrong place and the water may go down the steps instead. Every dragon who gets home on sound footing has benefited from someone knowing the difference. Istra knows it. I am very fond of her for that and for the fact that she finished the inspection while showing me her town. There’s something immensely reassuring about being recognised as a creator and asked to wait while somebody does the important part of her morning.

Eggs and the warm hall

A dragon begins inside an egg. If you have only seen the shell from a distance, it’s worth knowing what an egg looks like when there’s a dragon nearly ready to come out. The shell may shift a little in its bed. You may hear a scrape before you see a crack. Once a claw comes through, it may disappear again and emerge from a different angle. A hatchling has to make room to move her shoulder as well as her head. Watch long enough and you’ll see why an opening that looks quite large to the rest of us may still take some work.

The warm hall on the ridge stands where dragons have been tending eggs for centuries. The keepers lay them in beds of warm gravel and leave enough room to walk between the beds. The gravel holds warmth around the lower part of the shell. Vents let the keepers manage the air. A screen gives visitors somewhere to watch from, and it helps keep somebody’s aunt from deciding that the best way to admire an egg is to breathe directly over it. I sympathise with the aunt. I have, at various times, been the aunt.

A keeper named Meri showed me why the room needs attention even when everything looks quiet. A tile had come loose and air was escaping. She had a strip of wool near the vent. It leaned with the draught, so she could see where the air wanted to go. I could warm the current while Istra fitted a spare tile. Meri watched the glass tube set in the wall and told me when she wanted more heat and when she wanted less. I’ve warmed eggs with my hands. She knew that room in a way I couldn’t have learnt by making it warm once. I was happy to follow her instructions, and I’m told there are people in several regions who would like that statement put somewhere they can point to it.

Here it is. I was happy to follow her instructions. She was looking after dragons.

Newly hatched, a dragon can have strong feet before she has wings that will carry her. The little one I watched in the warm hall braced herself against the nest with her hind feet. Her wings were still short folds. Meri cleared the wet membrane from her nose once she’d had a moment to catch her breath, then set down a warm cloth where she could crawl out of the draught. The hatchling dragged herself towards it, put her whole head underneath, and stayed there. I’d spent some time wondering how far she might fly when she grew. She was already making very sensible arrangements for the next five minutes.

Upstairs, warm water ran through a copper pipe to a room where young dragons could exercise the muscles along their wings. Some would fly readily once they were strong enough. Some would take longer. Others would prefer the water and might choose to go down to the low valleys. I like that there’s room to find out. When the first dragons hatched, Selena and I could watch them for signs that their bodies were doing what we hoped. Now a dragon can grow up among people who will help her learn what her body does well and show her several places she might go with it. Her choice can surprise the whole family. I think that’s a very good use of a childhood.

The dragons that carry you

The dragon trains are the part of dragon life most of you have seen for yourselves. Carrying passengers was the dragons’ idea. I am happy to claim a small share of the wings. If you wish to complain about the seat, you’ll have to speak to the person who sold you the ticket. If you’ve chosen the seat directly above a bundle of onions, you may also wish to speak to the person who taught you to make decisions by price alone.

A passenger route takes considerably more than a dragon who can fly the distance. Someone has to know what the air does at either end of the journey. A lovely, steady current halfway across the valley will be of limited comfort if the wind at the landing keeps driving you towards a wall.

Someone needs to judge the load, decide where to fasten it, and check that nothing can work loose as the dragon turns. If you’re asked to shift your luggage towards the middle, do it. Hold your own trunk at arm’s length for a minute and you’ll have some sympathy for the trouble a little weight can cause when you put it in the wrong place.

Coming in to land, a dragon can spread her wings wide and change their angle to slow herself. With more weight aboard, she needs room and time to make that approach. Give her both. If you’re waiting on the ridge and there are white bands on the stone, look at where the people who work there are standing. Istra waited for two dragons to clear an open strip before she crossed. She knew what they needed from her. You can pay a dragon the same courtesy even if all you know about her is the time she’s due.

I’m proud of the routes. A village across a mountain can get fresh supplies before the ground road is passable. A person can go to work on one side and come home to the other. You can visit somebody you miss and sit at her table before the evening’s over. That last one is my favourite. I helped make a creature that could cross a valley. The dragons have made it possible for people to keep loving somebody on the other side of one without spending half the year saying they’ll come when the snow melts.

What they remember

I expected I might have to introduce myself when I went to the ridge. A young rope seller looked at my face and then looked at Istra. A baker knew me from a song his father had sung while kneading. Two little dragons recognised me from a carving that held open their nursery door. I should like you to consider the amount of affection it takes for me to hear that last one and feel pleased. I do. A nursery door ought to stay open when somebody has her arms full. I’m glad the carving has found steady work.

Istra could trace the stories back through her mother and grandfather and a dragon who taught him to tend eggs. That was centuries in one family. The neighbouring family had its own stories and could tell you who had passed each one down. The dragons knew me as one of their creators. They’d also spent all that time becoming people I was only just getting to know. The rope seller let me hold one end of a rope while she showed me a knot. I was glad she trusted me with it. There are only so many ways to respond to being recognised, and I prefer those that give me something to do with my hands.

When Meri asked whether I’d put my name beside the time of the hatching, I wrote it. Then I added Istra’s and asked Meri for hers. I had been there when the egg opened. Istra had made sure the warm air stayed where it belonged. Meri had been tending the eggs since before daylight. If that hatchling ever reads the slate, I hope she’ll see how much company she had before she knew how to look for it.

That’s what I wanted to tell you about the dragons. You can know how the wing works, what makes a good landing, and why the gravel around an egg has to stay warm. I want you to know those things. The next time you see one, I hope you’ll look long enough to notice what she’s doing with the body she grew into and the life she’s made. She may be carrying you somewhere.

She may be going home from work with a rake over her shoulder. She may be small enough to sleep on your sleeve, in which case you’re in for a long afternoon and I envy you every minute of it.

Druids

If you want to know whether someone is a Druid, give her a houseplant. You’ll learn whether she likes houseplants, which is a perfectly good thing to know about somebody. You’ll still have to ask her whether she’s a Druid.

I’ve been given several houseplants by people who expected a demonstration. The one I remember best was a basil plant Selena brought home because she wanted basil. I made it grow large enough to shade the kitchen window. She cut what she needed, put the rest outside, and asked me to explain why I’d made it impossible to see whether it was raining.

The basil was doing wonderfully. I’d overlooked the window. If a Druid had been there, I hope she would have asked why we wanted the basil before I began. We needed six leaves.

That gets us surprisingly close to the point. You can make a great deal happen in a living place if you have the power. A Druid pays attention to what’s already happening there, and to what will keep happening after she goes home. I have known Druids who could tell you more by putting a hand against a bank and waiting than I would have learnt by lifting the bank out of the ground and turning it over. I would have had a lovely view of the underside. I would also have had a river running through my shoes.

What I mean by Druid

The first thing you’ll notice about a Druid may be that you’ve already walked past her. There’s no particular height I can give you, and a cloak will tell you mainly whether she expected it to be cold. If she’s been out on wet ground, look at what she does with her boots before she comes in. Someone who takes care to leave the mud outside is welcome at my table regardless of what she calls herself. I’d be grateful if you passed that along to anyone who has visited me recently and recognised their own footprints in this paragraph.

There’s an old distinction I want to keep clear. True-born Druids belong to a people with a particular connection to the living world. Learning to read the woods can make you an excellent woodskeeper. Learning to cast a spell that helps an injured tree can make you a very welcome neighbour to somebody who loves that tree. A Druid can learn either skill, and so can people who were born without the connection I’m describing. I’ve met people who would take offence at that distinction in both directions. If you’re one of them, I suggest planting a tree with somebody from the other side and seeing how long either of you can keep the argument going while you share a spade.

Having a connection to the living world should not be confused with having command of it. The distinction matters to me because people hear that a Druid can notice a change and assume she

can also prevent it. Reyla’s life gives that mistake consequences. Sensing, understanding, and deciding how to act are separate things, even when the same person does all three.

You can see part of the distinction in how a Druid listens. Suppose she puts her palm against a tree. At first glance, she’s standing with her hand on a piece of bark, which is exactly what you’re doing if you try it beside her. You might feel ridges under your fingertips, heat where the sun has reached the trunk, or moisture in a cleft. Those are worth noticing. Give yourself a moment before you decide you’ve got everything you can from the exercise. You may even feel movement when the wind puts weight on a branch.

A Druid can attend to the living processes beneath what you can touch. The tree draws water, sends what it makes in its leaves to places that need it, grows in one direction and endures damage in another. Its roots are in ground that takes water differently from one patch to the next. A Druid may pick up on how some of those things affect one another. The tree can tell her quite a lot about the place where it stands because it has been living with the consequences for years. When I say a tree can tell her something, I mean she can learn from that connection. If she tells you the tree said your cousin ought to pay her back, I’d be interested to hear the tree’s side of it. I’d also be interested to know how much your cousin owes her. The facts may be related.

How the connection works

You’re welcome to try listening the ordinary way. Go somewhere you can hear running water and stand still. Give yourself enough time to tell a single sound from the rest. There may be a quick tapping where water hits a loose stone, a low rush farther off, and a hollow sound where it passes underneath the bank. Once you’ve picked out one of those sounds, you can follow it when the others get louder. A Druid’s attention can work in much the same way. There’s a lot going on, and learning where to put your attention matters as much as having something there to hear. With a hand against bark, a Druid might first attend to the tree itself. If she follows the disturbance farther, she may find that the place where it shows is some distance from the place where it began. A branch can be the first part to show the strain. The cause may be below ground, on the far side of the trunk, where water has begun running away from roots that used to get their share. Give the branch all the help you like and the roots will still need water by evening. I recommend finding that out before you make the branch twice as big. I make this recommendation with the authority of a woman who has made a number of things twice as big.

“Was the basil the first?” Selena asks.

“No. It was the first that got in the way of you seeing the rain.”

“I meant the first plant.”

“So did I, my love. I was giving you a chance to narrow it further.”

You get better at listening by being wrong and finding out where. One wet patch can lead you to suppose the whole bank has had enough water. Dig a little farther down and you may find that the top is wet and the soil below is dry. A tree may be managing well in one season and badly in the next. A Druid who knows the ground can put what she senses beside what she knows about

the last flood, the length of the dry spell, or the ditch somebody cleared last spring. I trust the one who asks when the ditch was cleared. There’s a fair chance she’ll save us a morning.

A Druid has limits. The place has limits too. Give somebody who’s never heard the sea a roomful of people speaking and a shell with a faint sound in it, and see how readily she can tell you what she’s hearing. It helps to get closer. It helps to know what you’re listening for. It helps a great deal if everyone else will be quiet. If a Druid asks for a moment before answering, you can give her one. People have a curious habit of filling that particular moment with the question they just asked.

It matters which part of the process we are talking about. Noticing a living thing in distress is not the same as knowing its cause. Knowing the cause is not the same as being able to put it right. I would not have you expect a Druid to command every tree, root, and creature simply because she can sense something I would miss. A gift still requires attention, judgment, and sometimes the willingness to say she does not know.

Where they came from

The Druids were already a people when the elves came to know them. I’ve said that the dragons and the elves were the first born. The Druids belong to the early world too, and by the time the elves met them, they already had something of their own to bring to the meeting. I think that last part matters. If you tell the account entirely from the point of view of the people arriving, it’s easy to make the people already there sound as though they’d spent all their time waiting to be discovered. I have been discovered quite often. I can assure you I was usually in the middle of something.

People ask me what the Druids were made to do. I can give you a better answer if we separate what they were able to do from what they decided to do with it. Their connection made it possible for the living world to reach them in ways that it didn’t reach everyone. They could bring that awareness to a decision about where to build, when to leave a place alone, or how to help something recover. The decision still belonged to the person making it. Having a gift for listening to a river gives you excellent reasons to be consulted about the bridge. It also gives you excellent reasons to go home after the meeting and want supper without anybody asking about a second bridge.

To understand what that gift gave the early world, picture two settlements sharing the same water. One has a stretch of dry ground it wants to plant. The other depends on a shallow channel that stays wet only while enough water comes down from upstream. Both can tell you what they need. Somebody who can follow the water through the ground may be able to tell them which of their plans can work together. She can show them where changing the flow will matter, and where they might get what they need by doing something smaller. There’s a great deal of power in being able to say, “Come here. Feel how cool this soil is. You already have water closer to your fields than you think.”

That’s a practical example of what a Druid can offer. An elf may come to the same place with a different kind of magic, a different way of reading the ground, or knowledge about what can be

made there. People like to ask which of those gifts is greater. I like to ask what they’re trying to do. If you need your bank to hold through the next rain, you may be quite pleased to have both people looking at it. You’ll be more pleased if they like each other enough to share what they find.

The people who wanted the power

There are parts of this chapter I would rather be writing under different circumstances. I’d like to tell you where to go to meet several true-born Druids and hear them disagree with my account. I’d like there to be enough of them for somebody to write to me and say I’ve described a practice peculiar to the people upriver as if it were known everywhere. People often correct me about things I was present for. I’d be delighted to receive those letters.

The old Druid people were hunted and destroyed. Later, the government made claims about Druid power that people had little opportunity to check against a living Druid. A man took Druid blood in the pursuit of power. He acquired power through what he did, and he remained what he was. I want you to remember both parts of that because it’s very easy to hear that something worked and give it credit for doing everything the person who attempted it said it would do. A crystal can hold energy. A weapon can be made to spend it. Put a weapon in the hands of someone who has no power of her own and she can still use what was put into it. That tells you what the person who prepared the weapon managed to accomplish. To understand a Druid, you need to look at the connection that’s present when she kneels beside the water with empty hands. She can attend to something beyond herself and learn from what is happening there. There’s very little resemblance between that and pointing a device at the bank and waiting to see what gives way.

If you ever have to read a government record about Druids, pay attention to what was measured. A reading of how much energy moved can be accurate yet leave out almost everything a Druid would want to know. What did the movement do to the place it passed through? What lived there? Had anyone checked on it afterwards? I’m especially fond of that last question. It’s the sort of thing a person asks when she expects the place to have a life beyond the moment it was useful to her.

I’ll say one more thing before I go on. There were Druids who knew how to do things we can no longer ask them about. There were also Druids who were very bad at cooking, who lent things to the wrong neighbour, or who had favourite seats and thought everyone else knew it. I have no trouble believing all of that because I knew people then. I want you to leave room for their ordinary lives when you think of what was taken. A people loses rather more than its secrets when it loses the person who knew exactly how much water to put in the bread.

Reyla

Reyla is the last true-born Druid. If you’ve met her, I hope you’ll think of her face before you think of that sentence. I think of the way she could give something her attention without making you feel as if she’d stopped being aware of you. You could stand beside her while she listened, and there was room for you to notice what you noticed. If I offered an opinion too quickly, she

had a way of letting me hear the rest of it in my own head. On the better days I’d hear the mistake before I got it out of my mouth. I was always very pleased with myself on those days, which gave her another thing to be patient about.

On the road to Everlangyrs, Reyla listened to a tree. The roads and distances were still difficult to judge in the wake of the Fracture. A map could tell you how a place had once joined up with the next one. A tree had been living through what happened to the ground where it stood. The river offered a route. Those things were there for anyone to see, and Reyla could learn from them in a way that gave the rest of us more to go on. When I tell you a Druid can help people find their way, that’s the kind of help I mean. She can bring you to a better understanding of where you are, which is worth quite a lot when where you are used to be somewhere else.

Her work with settlements had much the same quality. A place can look as if it needs saving when what the people there need most is time and help making a decision. A stream has been rising farther up its bank every year. Somebody wants a wall. Somebody else remembers how badly the last wall turned the water against a neighbour’s field. The people have good reasons to disagree. A Druid can help them understand what’s going on under the part they can see. Then they can decide together what they’re prepared to build, move, or give up. Reyla could stay with that work. It takes a particular kind of patience to come back after the decision and ask how it’s going, especially when you suspect the people who argued longest have quietly changed what they’re doing.

Some of you know what Reyla chose to become. I think about that choice often. A reader who finds this chapter a hundred years from now might know her chiefly through the world she’s bound herself to. I remember her as somebody you could speak to over a table. Both are part of her life. What she chose for herself belongs to her. If you want to understand Druids through Reyla, pay attention to the way she listened while she could still lift her hand from the bark, turn to you, and ask what you’d heard.

What they leave in your hands

There’s a useful question to ask when you’ve spent time with a Druid. Ask what you ought to look for when you come back. She might tell you to watch whether the water reaches a certain stone after a night of rain. She might show you where the bark has started closing over a wound, so you know what healing looks like when the change is slow. You can do that much yourself. Bring somebody with you the next time and you can show her what you’ve been watching. I imagine a great deal of what the old Druids knew travelled that way. I wish I’d gone back to more places with more of them while I had the chance.

I’m looking out at the kitchen window now. Selena has put a pot of basil on the sill, and I can see the rain beyond it. I’ve counted the leaves she’s likely to want for supper. There are enough. I’d have liked to ask Reyla whether she thought that would do. I expect she would have told me to look again tomorrow.

Dwarves

The first thing I’d like you to know about dwarves is that you can buy a very good hinge from one. The second is that you can buy a very bad hinge from one, particularly if you walk into the shop insisting you know exactly what you want. A dwarf who asks what you mean to hang from it is giving you a chance to save yourself some trouble. Take the chance. It’s included in the price, whereas coming back on a wet afternoon to explain that you meant a stone door may cost you something.

There is another reason I begin with the hinge. It makes a Dwarf’s work visible in somebody else’s daily life. A good fitting can mean a gate closes safely in bad weather; a careful repair can keep a household from having to replace a door. Those are consequences you can see without deciding that every Dwarf must be a smith or that every settlement ought to look like a mine. A people cannot be described by a single workshop, no matter how well I like its owner.

“Did you tell her it was stone?” Selena asks.

“You can lift a house.”

“I thought she’d appreciate the compliment.”

Who you’re looking at

Dwarves come in the usual range of heights. If you ask me how to spot the dwarf in a crowded market, I’ll ask which dwarf you’re looking for. Iven, for instance, is considerably easier to find if you know his name. I’ve had excellent results with names, even in markets where nobody was particularly keen to see me.

People want a description they can use from across the street. I can give you one for a particular dwarf once I know whom you mean. Tell me you’re looking for the woman who sold you a silver clasp and I may be able to send you to the right stall. Tell me she was wearing a blue apron and I’ll ask whether the blue was still visible below the waist. Some kinds of work are very hard on an apron. I remember the people who’ve done beautiful work for me much more readily than I remember the colour they wore while doing it.

If you like looking at hands, as I do, there’s a great deal to see in a workshop. The heel of one hand may be harder than the other. A tool can wear a smooth patch against the side of a finger.

Someone who files metal at a bench can come home with a pale stripe across her apron where she’s been leaning into the work. How they came to be

Dwarves belong to the world Selena and I made. As for the first dwarf, I cannot point to a particular morning and say, “There she is, and this is what I made her to do.” You might have heard a more definite account from somebody who wants to sell you a hammer. I’d ask to see the hammer before I gave much thought to the account.

I can tell you how a craft becomes part of a people’s life. Suppose a family settles where there’s good stone for building. At first, they take what they can use from the surface. By the time somebody has learnt to split a block cleanly, somebody else wants one cut to fit a doorway. The person who can cut it to fit gets asked again. A child watches long enough to notice that her aunt always wets the face of the stone before she marks it. She asks why. The aunt tells her, or hands her the chalk and lets her find out. A few generations later, a visitor comes through and says the people there have always known how to build in stone. By then even the family may have forgotten the first doorway.

You’ll find evidence of that kind of learning in the places people leave behind. A recess cut into the wall of a passage can keep tools clear of the path. Steps worn along one edge tell you where somebody actually walked with a load. An old bench may have holes where the vice used to be. I like a bench with several sets of holes. Somebody cared enough about the work to keep moving things until she was comfortable. If you set the vice back in the first pair just because they’re older, I hope her granddaughter catches you.

The skills travel, too. A dwarf who grew up around mines may go to the coast and find that what she knows about stone helps her decide where to cut a stair down to the water. The person who taught her may never have seen the sea. A family that has been working metal for generations may produce someone who uses her knowledge of heat to make a better oven and is happy to leave the knives to her sister. I have great respect for the sister, and I’d like an introduction to the one with the oven.

Under the mountain

You smell a working mine before you’ve gone very far into it. Wet stone has a smell of its own. Add lamp oil, old timber, and whatever the last person brought in for lunch and you can often tell how recently a passage has been used. The air changes as you go. A little movement across the back of your hand can tell you air is coming through a shaft. You may feel it at one turn and lose it at the next. An experienced miner notices where that happens. She’s been there on days when the air moved differently, and she’ll have an idea where to look if something’s changed. A miner learns how to move in a tight passage without catching her tools on the walls. She keeps the load close, checks where her feet are, and knows how much room she needs before she swings a hammer. If you hear a loud knock followed by a goddess saying she meant to do that, you’ve just heard the difference between having a hammer and knowing where to swing it. Watch somebody who knows the stone. One face may split along a clean line. A few steps farther on, the same-looking rock may come away in awkward plates that sound different under a light strike. The sound tells her something. The shape of the break tells her more. She still has to decide where the loose piece will go if she frees it. I’m very good at holding things in the air. The last time I offered to demonstrate, the woman showing me the passage said she’d tell me if she needed anything held. She gave me the lamp. It was precisely what she needed held, which made it unusually difficult to complain.

Water is another thing a person learns to notice underground. It can follow a slope small enough that you feel level while you’re walking it. A drainage groove has to lead somewhere. Cut it too shallow where the passage dips and you’ve made a trough. Leave a little lip at the outlet and water can back up where people have to walk through it. Somebody has to find that lip, usually by getting her feet wet and noticing that the water is considerably better at staying put than the plans suggested.

There’s work in getting stone out of a mountain, and there’s work in keeping the mountain above the people doing it. The first earns a person a living. The second gives her a reasonable chance of getting home to spend it. If a dwarf who’s been showing you a seam tells you she wants to look at a support before she goes any farther, wait where she puts you. You can admire her competence from there. I’ve found that a pleasant way to spend time with a woman who has asked me to stand still. It does help if you actually stand still.

What comes out of the workshop

A useful object has to suit the hand that will use it. Pick up two tools with different handles and pretend you’ll have to use each until supper. The one that feels pleasant for the first few minutes

may make you pinch your fingers to stop it turning. The other may feel too thick until you put on the gloves you wear for the work. Once the maker knows about your gloves, your grip, and the part of the job where the handle gets wet, she has a better chance of giving you something you’ll still like by the end of the day.

A dwarf making a knife for somebody who cleans fish on a boat might ask to see how that person stands when she works. There’s a difference between the movement you make at a table and the one you make when the floor moves under you. A handle with enough purchase for a wet hand may matter more to her than an ornament that will impress her neighbours at the market. She can have both. She’ll pay for both. If the ornament is a pair of little silver fish, I’d pay for them too, and then I’d spend all afternoon holding the knife up for Selena to see until she asked whether I intended to clean a fish with it.

Stonework asks different questions. You can cut a stone so it fits its neighbour closely on the day it’s laid. You also have to think about where the weight will come down, how the ground may shift, and where water can get in. The smooth face is the part visitors will admire. The fitting surfaces are the parts you’ll be grateful for after the first hard winter. A maker can take great pleasure in both. You can tell when she does by how much trouble she takes with the part she knows you’ll never see again.

Jewellery gives you a different way to see that care. Run a finger along the back of a brooch. A rough edge there can catch a thread every time the cloth moves. A pin needs enough spring to sit firmly in its catch, and a stone needs to be held so it stays in place when somebody reaches across a table or gets caught in the rain. Then there’s the part where the maker decides how the whole thing ought to look on you. I like that part very much. There are few greater pleasures than having somebody choose where a piece of silver ought to catch the light and discovering that you agree with her about where to look.

A well-made clasp keeps a cloak on. A beautiful clasp gives you a reason to look at the place where the cloak fastens. Both are legitimate achievements. If I spend an extra moment fastening a particularly fine clasp at Selena’s throat, that’s a compliment to the maker as well as to the woman wearing it.

“You fastened it to your own sleeve last time,” Selena says.

“I thought it held very well.”

“It did. We went halfway down the street that way.”

A well-made clasp, as I say.

Dwarves on the island

The island where Breni met Iven is a good place to look if you want to understand what skill with materials can do once it’s used against somebody. Some of the dwarves there used weapons made to work against vampire essence. A weapon like that depends on somebody understanding more than the shape of a blade. The maker has to find a way for what’s in the weapon to affect

what’s in the person it’s used against. That’s a particular kind of work, and it can do a particular kind of harm.

Think of the difference between making a key and making something to break a lock. Both makers need to know something about the lock. One uses that knowledge to move the parts in the right order. The other looks for the place that can be made to give way. I find a key an elegant bit of work. I’ve also met people who made breaking the lock sound terribly clever and then expected the person who owned the door to be impressed. It depends rather heavily on whose side of the door you’re standing on.

The island affair involved people trying to make vampires carry the blame for what was happening there. Once you understand that, you can see why the weapons matter beyond the immediate danger. Somebody had learnt enough about vampires to make a thing that could hurt them in a way other people couldn’t easily see or explain. The people involved could count on old suspicions to make their own account sound plausible. You can buy a great deal of time with a plausible account, particularly if the people who might contradict you are already in trouble. Iven was a dwarf too. He worked with Breni, and the people being held were freed. That’s one reason I use his name in this chapter. If you’ve heard about the dwarves on the island and imagine you know which side Iven took, you’ve been given a category where you needed a person’s name. Breni had to learn who she could work with. Iven had choices of his own to make. A great deal depended on what they did with what they learnt and whether they could trust each other long enough to do something with it.

I’m fond of Breni, as you may have noticed. I’m particularly fond of the way she can tell you what a dangerous object does and then ask who has the keys. I’m inclined to go on thinking about the object. She’s generally thinking about who’s still behind the door. I’ve found there are occasions when a goddess can be very useful once someone tells her exactly which door.

What travels with the goods

The role dwarves play in the world is easiest to see when something changes hands. A piece of metal leaves a mine. Somebody works it. Somebody carries it over water or down a road. A person at the other end puts it into use and may come back to ask for another. What travels along that route is knowledge as well as material. The person who sells a tool hears how it performed. The maker finds out what failed. The next person who buys one can benefit from a conversation she’ll never hear.

A coastal trader may want fittings that hold up in salt air. Somebody inland may need the same fitting to stand up to frost or a gate that’s left open in a strong wind. Both can walk into a shop and say they want something sturdy. If the dwarf behind the counter asks where you live, this is a reasonable explanation for the question. She may also like you. You can usually tell which by whether she asks how you get home after you’ve already paid.

There’s one more thing I like about trade. It gives you a reason to come back. You can start with a hinge, find that the person who made it knows where to get good lamp oil, and eventually be the person who brings her the oil when she’s too busy to fetch it. After enough years, somebody

will ask how your families met. One of you will remember the hinge and one of you will remember the oil. I see no reason to settle the disagreement if both of you still have an excuse to visit.

So if you find yourself in a dwarf’s shop, I suggest you begin by telling her what you came for. Give her the full description of the door, including anything you were hoping she wouldn’t charge extra for. If you find yourself admiring a pair of silver fish you have absolutely no use for, you may buy them anyway. I bought mine because Selena saw me pick them up, smiled, and asked what I meant to do with them. I said I’d think of something. The maker asked whether I wanted her to wrap them separately. I said yes before I knew why.

One is on Selena’s cloak. The other is on mine. We can walk down the street independently now, which I suppose is an improvement in the design. I still look for reasons to take her arm.

Elves

The elves and the dragons were the first ones born. I told you what it was like to have a dragon fall asleep on my sleeve. The elves had a different way of making sure I stayed where they wanted me. On the occasions I hadn’t, I was in considerable danger of trying it before supper. I loved them from the beginning. What you’ll see

An elf’s ears rise to a point, and the longer ones draw back far enough that you’ll see the full shape better in profile. Look where the ear joins the head and you can follow the rim up from the small folds near the opening to where it narrows. It’s a lovely line. I’ve been told I have an unfair interest in it by people who go to considerable trouble to wear their hair behind their ears. If you want me to believe you put that little gold ring there by accident, you’ll have to stop turning your head towards the lamp.

The length varies. So does the angle. Two elves standing side by side can have different ears in much the same way they can have different noses. You may find a resemblance between people in the same family. If you tell them about it, one of them will immediately point out something you missed. I recommend listening. You’ll learn more from somebody who’s lived with a face since it was small than from staring at it across a room and supposing you’ve worked out the history of the family.

I can tell you exactly how long an ear is and still leave out what it feels like to live with it. Consider a hood. If it’s cut too close, you may put it up in a hurry and catch the tip of your ear in

the seam. Leave enough room and you can pull it up over your head while you’re walking. The good ones stay where you put them when you turn to see who’s behind you. The bad ones turn with you and give you a fine view of the inside of a hood. I own several of the bad ones. They were very pretty on the peg, and I’ve never claimed the ability to see all the consequences of a purchase.

The shape of my own ears has led to a certain amount of speculation. I know what you’re looking at when you look at me, and you’re welcome to look. If you’re hoping to recognise every elf by deciding how much she resembles a goddess, you’ll have a hard morning. An elf can be tall. An elf can be short. What I hope you’ll imagine is the pleasure of seeing somebody you know well turn her head and give you something new to notice in a face you thought you knew by heart.

What we gave them

People ask what the elves were made for. I remember Selena and me making a world we hoped people would want to inhabit. That’s the closest thing I have to a short answer. The longer one begins with what we gave the elves and goes on to what they did with it. You’re quite right if you’re thinking that the second part could fill a book of its own. I’ve been enjoying the freedom to write only one chapter, and I intend to enjoy it until I catch myself writing another. We gave them bodies capable of living in the world and minds capable of wondering why it was the way it was. They had a capacity for magic from early on. A person who can feel power moving through something has a chance to learn where it goes, what holds it, and what might happen if she touches it. A person who asks why is likely to try the same thing a second time under different conditions. Give her enough years and she can become very good at explaining why the first time went the way it did. Give her a pupil and you may discover that half of what she knows is something she’s never had to put into words.

Here’s a small example of the kind of attention I mean. Say you have a lamp whose flame keeps going thin. You can put more power into it. It may burn brighter for a while. If you can feel where the power travels, you can follow it from the place it enters to the place the flame takes hold. You might find that the trouble begins somewhere in between. Now you have a choice. You can keep giving it more power whenever it dims, or you can find out what’s happening at that point and fix what’s causing the trouble. I’ve done both. The first way was very popular with me until I wanted to go to bed and discovered I’d made the lamp dependent on my continued interest.

A mage who has spent years studying how magic behaves can often see a useful difference between two problems that look identical to the person who brought them in. One lamp may be

losing what’s been put into it. Another may be getting more than it can use evenly. The owner has brought you two dim lamps. The work begins when you find out why they’re dim. I like this about elven magic. It gives a person a good reason to sit down, take something seriously, and tell me to wait until she’s finished.

“Do you wait?” Selena asks.

“For an elf who knows what she’s doing, certainly.”

“How long?”

“Long enough to find out whether she’d like a chair.”

Selena says I have also been known to offer a chair while somebody is already sitting in one. I see no need to name the elf in question. I remember her very well and hope she remembers me in much the same spirit.

What they learnt

If you’re wondering what an elf’s magic looks like, I’d ask what she’s using it for. There’s magic you can recognise from the other side of a field. I’ve made a fair amount of that. There’s also magic whose best result is that the person doing it can go home and tell you the afternoon passed quietly. With some kinds of work, a quiet afternoon is a very large achievement.

Selena sought out an old elf mage when she needed help bringing Taylor into the world. I want you to give the mage a moment of your attention before you move on to the birth. Selena could bring power to the work herself. She needed somebody who understood how to help when power was only one of the things that mattered. The mage had the experience to attend to what was happening as it happened, and to work with Selena through it. I find that moving. I also find it wonderfully practical. There are times when the loveliest thing anyone can say to you is that she knows what to do and has already washed her hands.

Think about what it takes to use magic around a life still coming into the world. The amount that was right a moment ago may need adjusting. The person you’re helping may feel a change before you see one. If she tells you what she feels, you have to listen even when it differs from what you expected. Experience gives you a better chance of knowing which small changes matter. It also teaches you how long to wait before deciding that your help has helped. If you’ve ever taken the lid off a pot every few breaths to see whether it’s started boiling, you may understand why I admire that last skill in other people.

The old mage had learnt things Selena could ask her to do. I don’t know how many of those things she learnt from somebody older, how many she had to work out herself, or which one she was proudest of. I wish I could ask her. I’ve heard her described by what she did for our family, which is how you know why I remember her. A person can do a great thing for you and have spent most of her life being important to people you’ll never meet. I think she’s owed that much space in the account.

The world they lived in

The elves came to know the Druids in the early world. I’d like you to think of that meeting from both sides. The elves had their own ways of using magic. The Druids could attend to the living world in a way the elves had reason to be curious about. There’s a pleasure in finding out that the person beside you knows something you can’t do, particularly once you’ve got over the irritation of finding out how much you’d like to be able to do it yourself. I’ve had both feelings, sometimes while Selena was still demonstrating the thing in question.

I described the Druid’s connection in the last chapter but one. Consider the tree there again. A Druid may be able to follow the life of the tree into the ground it’s drawing from. An elf who studies magic may bring a different question to the same tree. She may want to know where power introduced at one point will travel, or whether it can reach the place she means it to reach without disturbing the growth around it. Put the two of them together and there’s a chance that one will ask a question that changes what the other is looking for. If they’re patient with each other, they may both go home knowing something they couldn’t have found out alone.

The world was already old enough to have known the war between the Ogres and the Giants before the elves and Druids met. It helps to remember that when I call the elves one of the first peoples. A people can be very old and still be new to a particular place. They may arrive somewhere that has its own history, its own trouble, and people who have been living through the consequences for longer than the visitors can guess. If they’re wise, they’ll find somebody willing to tell them what sort of place they’ve entered. If they’re lucky, that person will be willing to tell them over supper. I prefer to be lucky. Supper has saved me from making several mistakes while hungry and therefore quite certain I was right.

Keep the order straight, because a story can become misleading just by placing the right events in the wrong sequence: the war between Ogres and Giants belonged to the earlier world, and the meeting of Elves and Druids came afterwards. That tells you when the events stand in relation to one another. It does not, by itself, tell you what began the war or how every group remembered its ending.

Later, elven history took more than one course. I’ve already given the Dark Elves their own chapter. They have their own accounts of what mattered to them and why. I’d like you to remember that when you come across somebody who thinks the entire history of elven peoples can be worked out from the shape of an ear. You can follow an ancestry as far as it goes and still need to ask a person where she grew up, whom she learnt from, and what she calls home. Those questions become particularly important once the world has shifted the ground beneath the answers.

What it means to be the last

Fairies

If a fairy asks you to dance, find out which floor she means. The one under your feet is a reasonable guess. I’ve made it myself. I’ve also come home with mud halfway up my stockings after leaving the house on a perfectly dry evening, so you may judge how much use my experience has been to me.

“Were those my stockings?” Selena asks.

“They were by the door.”

“I know where I put them.”

“Then you’ll be pleased to hear I brought them back to exactly the right house.”

I see I’m going to have to explain the bit in between. Fortunately, that’s what a chapter on fairies is for.

A fairy at arm’s length

The fairies I’m talking about here can be small enough to stand on an open hand. Offer the hand palm up and let her decide whether to take it. You’ll feel her feet settle separately, and if she’s been flying for a while, you may feel a little warmth where she sits. Keep the hand steady. People are very good about that until somebody they like walks into the room. Then they turn their whole arm to say hello, and the fairy has to find out how much warning she gets before the ground changes direction.

I enjoy looking at their wings when they’re folded. You can follow the fine supports from where they join the back out towards the rim. The surface between them is thin enough to let light through. A wing that looks nearly clear against a window can show a wash of amber or green when the fairy turns away from it. The colour may change again before you’ve decided what to call it. I recommend getting a good look while she’s comfortable. If you keep asking her to turn so you can settle an argument about green, there’s a fair chance the last colour you see will be the back of her coat.

In flight, those same wings can make enough movement for you to feel it against your cheek. In a quiet room you’ll hear a fine buzz as she gathers herself to rise. Put her outside by the water, with reeds scraping together and frogs making their contributions, and you may lose that sound entirely. You can still see how much she’s doing with her body. She tips forward to go forward. As she comes in to land, she brings herself more upright and opens her hands if there’s something she wants to catch. If that something is your earring, you’ll learn how well you attached it.

A fairy can fit under a spray of leaves where the air is fairly still. A few inches farther out, a gust can take hold of her before it does much more than move your hair. That makes low flights through sheltered places quite sensible. Watch a fairy go down a hedge and you may see her keep just beneath the leaves on the exposed side, then come out where the branches make a break in the wind. If you’re following along the path, it can look as though she’s taking an unnecessarily winding route. She has a better view of the wind than you do. She can see what it’s doing to the leaf she’s about to pass, and she has considerably more at stake in the answer.

A body that size

When people ask how I’d go about making a fairy, they generally expect me to start with a person and make her smaller. I’d begin by asking where she was going to live. A drop of rain that runs down your sleeve can soak a small set of wings. A flower that moves very little while you’re looking at it from the path may swing half a fairy’s height when she lands on one side of it. Then there’s the problem of putting a good-sized door handle on a house whose front room would fit inside your bread box.

“Would you like me to move the bread box?” Selena asks.

“I’ve barely looked at it.”

“You opened the lid when you said ‘front room.’”

I wanted to see how the light fell. It’s very good in there until somebody shuts the lid. That’s the sort of thing you want to establish before you promise anybody a house.

A fairy’s size gives her ways through the world that the rest of us scarcely see. The underside of a bridge can offer a sheltered route along the water. The thick roots of a tree can make a dry landing under the bank. A gap between two boards might be wide enough for her to go through with her wings folded, but too narrow for her to open them if there’s a drop immediately on the other side. She needs to know what’s beyond the opening before she commits herself to it. I’m interested in that because it affects where you’ll find the same fairy when the weather changes. She may come through a particular gap every summer and use the long way round once the boards swell in the rain.

Cold matters more when you’re small enough to lose warmth quickly. So does finding something to eat on a journey. If you’ve put out a place for a fairy to rest, shelter it from the wind. If you’ve invited her to eat, offer her some of what everyone else is having, in a portion she can manage. I once saw someone provide a fairy guest with a single berry and then eat a hot dinner directly in front of her. She asked him for a potato. He brought her a whole one on a saucer. She ate what she wanted, asked for something to wrap the rest in, and went home with enough to feed the other people who’d been left off his dinner invitation.

I wish I’d thought to ask whether she did it on purpose. I like to think she did. That may be because I’ve been served a single berry by people who wanted to see what a goddess would do with it, and I’m still thinking of good answers.

How a circle works

A fairy circle is a crossing made by fairies working together. You may also hear somebody call a ring of mushrooms a fairy circle. There’s no harm in the name. It’s the reason some people spend all evening standing among mushrooms waiting for a door to open. If the mushrooms have enjoyed the company, they’ve been very quiet about it.

It is tempting to ask me for a tidy list: how many fairies, how far, how long, and how much can cross. The work I have described gives you a firmer answer to a different question. A circle depends on the people keeping the shared pattern and knowing where they intend to open it. I will not put a number on its reach or its capacity just because a number would fit neatly in the margin of this page.

The circles I mean are made by people who can put their magic into the same movement. One fairy begins the rhythm. The others take it up until each can feel where her part fits. The line they make around the crossing needs to hold while the people using it pass through. You can mark that line on the ground, but the chalk is there to help everybody keep their place. I could draw you a perfect circle in chalk and leave it there all week. At the end of the week I’d have a chalk circle and, if it was on the kitchen floor, a wife who’d found another use for my toothbrush. Think of several people lifting a basket between them. If everyone lifts when she’s ready, the basket may tip before it comes off the ground. Once they agree on the moment to lift, each can take her share of the load. The circle needs that sort of agreement through more than one movement. A step puts a fairy where she needs to be. The rhythm tells her when the others will be there with her. She can adjust a little when somebody’s slow, provided the others can feel the adjustment and move with it. That’s one reason a group who’ve worked together for years can seem to be having a fairly easy time of something that makes a new group furious by the end of the afternoon.

Singing helps where people can hear one another. In a place with a great deal of echo, the sound that comes back may arrive late enough to confuse somebody who’s new to the crossing. The people who know the place can use the movement of the person beside them, or use a beat clear enough to pick out from what comes back off the rock. If you’ve been told to watch a fairy’s hand, watch it. She has given you something you can follow even if a waterfall starts making a larger contribution than anybody asked for.

Here’s the detail people often leave out. The people making the circle need to know where they’re opening it. Getting the rhythm right gives them a crossing they can hold. It doesn’t tell them whether the ground at the far end is dry. Someone has to know the far side well enough to choose where you’ll arrive and what you’ll have room to do when you get there. If you’re crossing with a basket, that may be as simple as having a clear patch of ground on which to put the basket down. If you’re bringing several people who’ve never crossed that way, it helps if the first one can move aside before the next arrives. I have been the second one. I learnt quite a lot about the first one’s elbows.

Why they keep the rhythm

People who hear singing and see a ring of dancers tend to remember the prettiest part. I do too. There’s a point in a well-kept circle when you can see the light at the rim gather between one fairy and the next. On damp ground it can show in the water around a blade of grass. Look towards the middle and you may be able to see what’s on the other side. I like the first moment when you can make out a shadow falling in a direction it couldn’t fall where you’re standing. It takes a moment for your eye to find the light that belongs to it.

Ask a fairy how long that moment lasts and she may tell you it depends on how well everybody slept. She’ll have other answers if you keep asking. A circle takes attention to keep open. The people doing it have to stay with the beat even while somebody at the edge asks whether they can go back for their hat. They may have made room for a certain number of crossings and need to close when those are done. The longer you stand deciding whether to go, the longer somebody is holding the way for you. Ask your questions before you get to the middle, where possible. If you discover a question in the middle, make it a short one.

A circle can also tell you something about how fairies teach their young. You can practise the movement without opening anything. A learner can stand beside somebody who knows the rhythm and find where she loses it. The others can repeat the part she needs, or slow it down until she can feel which step leads to the next. Once she can follow, she can try giving the beat herself. That’s a different skill. She has to keep track of what the others are ready for while doing the movement she’s asking them to follow. I enjoy watching somebody realise she’s doing it. There’s usually a little glance around the group to see whether anybody else has noticed. They generally have.

There are evenings when a group dances for the pleasure of it. Please allow them those. A baker who dances after work is entitled to finish a song without being asked for bread. The same applies to a fairy who knows how to open a crossing. If she offers to show you what she does, you can be pleased she wants you there. If she asks you to dance, you can be pleased about that too. I am, on occasion, pleased enough to forget to ask about the floor. I imagine this will continue.

What a crossing is good for

A dragon can carry a great deal across open country. A fairy circle can give people a way between places where there’s scarcely room to turn around. Picture a narrow clearing above a stream. The ground road runs along the bank, and a hard rain has taken part of it. On the far side

there’s a household waiting for someone to bring word that the rest of their family is safe. Somebody could try the long road. If fairies know a usable place on both sides and can hold the crossing long enough, they could get the message there in the time it takes to walk through the circle.

I’d count that as useful work even if all that crossed was a scrap of paper. It might matter more to the person waiting than a whole cart of supplies will matter to somebody else. Think about the way small settlements stay in touch when a path closes. News of which way is still open can save the next traveller a wasted day. A small parcel can contain the one bit of a tool the owner can’t make at home. A person who knows how to get through can tell the people on both sides when they can expect to see each other again. Fairies have good reason to know the sheltered places and the short ways between them. A place you walk past without seeing can be the place from which they’ve been keeping everyone else in reach.

There’s a smaller use I like just as much. A fairy who can show you a clearing from one side of a crossing and take you to it from another can teach you something about where you live. You may have thought the wood behind your house ended at the stream. She may know who lives farther up it, where the water goes when it rises, and what flowers come out on the bank three weeks before you see any in your own garden. You can learn those things by walking too. The crossing gives you a reason to meet somebody who already knows which way to go.

When you get there, ask whether she’d like you to remember where the opening was. I mean that quite literally. There’s a difference between being invited somewhere and being given directions you can pass on to everybody at the next market. A resting place may be quiet because the people who use it know who’s coming. A sheltered bank may have very little room. You may be able to get there with a fairy who knows how to bring you through and find that a dozen people trying to follow your description have trampled the only dry place to stand. If you’re the person who brought the dozen, you’ll want to have brought something more substantial than apologies. I’d begin with a plank.

Before you step in

I can open a doorway on my own. I’m mentioning that because people ask whether I taught the fairies to open theirs by doing what I do. When I make one, I can hold the whole thing myself and change it while it’s open. Fairies give one another parts of the work. A fairy learning to do it has to learn when she can rely on the person beside her and how to let that person know she needs help. I enjoy watching that. There are entire conversations in a movement of the hand that would take me half a page to explain. As you’ve had some opportunity to discover, I am very willing to use half a page.

There are times when I wish I could put a difficult bit of magic down in the middle and ask somebody to hold my place while I look at it. I have Selena for some of that. She has a very good sense of when I’ve got hold of a thing and when I’m holding it because I don’t want anyone to see me put it down. If she says I can give her my end, I generally can. I imagine a fairy feels much the same when she looks across a circle at somebody she’s been working with since they were both small enough to think the rim of a cup was a high place.

That’s what I’d like you to look for if you’re invited to watch. The light is lovely. I can see why people remember it. Look at the hands as well. You may see one fairy give another the beat back after she’s lost it. You may see someone at the far side of the ring wait until the person beside her is ready. The crossing holds because they’re doing those things for each other. If they’ve done it well, you’ll have a moment in which to think about the place you’re going. Use it to look where you’ll put your feet.

Selena has left a clean pair of stockings beside my book. I’ve asked whether this means she’d like to go dancing again. She says it means she’d like me to wear my own.

She’s also put out two pairs of boots. I know an invitation when I see one, particularly when it comes with such a sensible opinion of the ground.

Giants

A Giant will sometimes offer to carry you up a mountain. If she does, ask where she means to put you. Her shoulder is a lovely place from which to admire the view. It’s also where she hangs her lunch, and if the wind’s behind you, the smell of her lunch may be the principal thing you remember about the mountain.

I like Giants. I like that you can hear one laugh from the far side of a hollow and still hear what she was laughing at when you get there. I like that a woman can set down a pack large enough for me to spend the night in, open three separate pockets, and tell me she’s forgotten the bread. I especially like the ones who remember I’m a goddess halfway through asking me to help with something and decide to finish asking. I’ve had much better afternoons that way than I’ve ever had standing where somebody told me the light looked divine.

If you want to understand Giants, begin with what a day costs them. Think about the food they carry, the ground they cross, the weight of their own bodies, and the trouble of doing something gently when the people around them are built on a different scale. Then look at what they’ve learnt to do with all of that. You’ll get farther than the man who wrote to ask how many ordinary people you could pile up to make one Giant. I asked him whether he’d got their permission. He hasn’t written back.

What a Giant looks like up close

You probably notice the height first. I do too, although I’ve spent a good deal of my life being the person people look down to speak to, and I find it saves time to look up before anyone has to ask. If you’re beside a Giant on a slope, you can sometimes get a better look by standing a little uphill. You’ll see the shape of her face without having to crane your neck. With luck, she’ll get a better look at yours. I’ve used the same arrangement for less scholarly purposes and can tell you it works very well.

Giants vary in build. A woman who carries loads over steep ground may have thick calves and shoulders that sit unevenly while she’s wearing her pack. Take the pack away and you can see whether the unevenness belonged to her or to whoever packed it. The hands are worth looking at. A finger can be broad enough to cover the head of a nail you’d have to steady between two fingertips. The nail is still a small thing in that hand. She can pick it up if she has a clear place to get hold of it. Scatter a dozen nails over rough stone and you can turn the job into a great nuisance for somebody who could carry the whole anvil home.

The size of a person’s tools tells you something about how she works. Look at the handle of a Giant’s knife and you may find enough room for her hand to settle without wrapping tightly around itself. The blade can be quite ordinary in proportion to the handle. Put the same knife in my hand and I’m likely to grip farther up than the maker intended. If the knife came from

someone’s pack, I’ll ask first. I’ve been asked to return a knife I was still holding and found it a very awkward moment to discover how little of the handle my fingers covered.

A Giant’s coat can give you a better idea of the body underneath than standing at a distance and trying to decide how many of you it would take to fill it. The cloth pulls differently across the back when she lifts both arms. The hem may be cut to clear her knees so it stays out of the way on a climb. Look at the cuffs if she works outdoors. On a coat that sees a lot of use, the cuff on the side she favours may be mended where she’s brushed it against rock or set it down against a load. If it’s a very beautiful coat, admire it before you ask about the mending. She may have done both herself and deserves the pleasure of telling you which took longer.

What size asks of the body

People talk about a Giant’s strength as if it arrives with nothing to do until there’s a cart in a ditch. It’s already busy. A Giant has to lift her own weight with every step up a slope. When she comes down, she has to check that same weight before it carries her farther than she meant to go. A foot set firmly on dry rock may do a very different job from a foot set on wet grass. The leg above it has to adjust quickly enough to keep the rest of her over it. If you’ve ever come down a hill with a heavy sack and found the last few steps happening faster than you’d planned, you know something of what she’s managing.

Making a body taller changes the demands on it. If you made yourself twice as tall and everything else grew in proportion, you’d have considerably more than twice as much of yourself to carry. There’d be more of you across the shoulders and from front to back as well as from the ground to the top of your head. That extra weight has to go somewhere when you stand. It goes down through the joints and into your feet, and then the ground has an opinion.

“Are you going to demonstrate?” Selena asks.

“I was considering it.”

“Outside, please.”

I have the good fortune to be married to a woman who knows the difference between discouraging my curiosity and saving the floor.

When Selena and I made a world that could sustain very large bodies, we had to think about what those bodies would need from the ground, the air, and the food available to them. I can describe those requirements more clearly than I can tell you what the very first Giant wanted to do with her afternoon. I hope it was something pleasant. I hope nobody saw her standing there and immediately asked her to shift a rock. The temptation is considerable, particularly if you’ve spent all morning trying to shift it yourself.

A strong back can carry a heavy load, provided the pack lets the weight sit where the body can support it. The same load hung farther out pulls the shoulders backwards and makes the person lean to compensate. That works for a few steps. Over a long climb, she’ll feel every inch the pack sits away from her. A broad strap can spread pressure, and a lower fastening can keep the load from rolling to one side. If you’re helping pack, ask where the heavy thing goes. A Giant

can certainly move it after you’ve put it in the wrong pocket. I’ve found she’s more inclined to invite you on the next walk if you spare her the pleasure.

What’s in the pack

Food, usually. If you asked me to describe a Giant travelling through the mountains and gave me one object to put beside her, I’d choose the pack before the weapon. The weapon tells you there’s something she’s prepared to meet. The pack tells you she expects to get hungry before she gets home. I find the second prediction more reliable, although I admit I’ve used lunch to prepare for a few encounters I could otherwise have faced in a perfectly pleasant mood. What travels well depends on the route. Fresh food can be worth carrying if you’ll eat it that day. Anything intended to last has to stand up to changes in weather and being jostled every time you step down from a ledge. A loaf packed next to a cooking pot may become a flat loaf. It will still feed you. A pot packed next to something that can break it may spend the rest of the trip finding new ways to sound expensive. I like a good wrapping cloth between the things I mean to eat and the things I mean to eat from. If it keeps the cheese out of the spare socks, I consider it to have justified the space.

On a climb, water can matter more than anything else in the pack. Carry all you’ll need and it costs you effort for as long as it stays on your back. Plan to fill up along the way and you have to know whether the spring you’re counting on still runs in the season you’re travelling. A Giant who knows a ridge can tell you where the water is likely to be and where she last saw it. Those are two different answers. You’ll appreciate the distinction if you arrive thirsty and find a hollow full of warm leaves.

You may see a spear, a bow, or another weapon secured where it can be reached without unpacking supper. Look at how it sits when the Giant moves. A long shaft can catch on an overhang when she ducks. It can swing into the person behind her when she turns. A good fastening makes room for both the weapon and the people on the path. Anyone can look formidable standing still with a spear. I’ve watched someone do it beautifully, then turn to accept a compliment and take the top off a bush. I gave her the compliment anyway. She’d already done enough to the bush.

There’s an advantage to travelling with a person who knows what her equipment will do before she needs it. A Giant who can take off her pack and stand it somewhere steady has a place to rest her shoulders and both hands free. If she’s brought food to share, ask what she wants you to get out. Don’t thrust your arm into the first pocket you see. A pocket large enough to hold lunch for a Giant is large enough to make you wonder, briefly and with great sincerity, whether you’ll be able to get your arm back. The answer may depend on whether you’re willing to let go of the cheese.

A path for more than one kind of traveller

A Giant can step over a break in the ground that would oblige me to jump. You might suppose that makes mountain travel easier for her everywhere. Give her a path cut against a cliff, with the wall leaning out over it, and see how much of her can use the ledge. I may have room to pass

with a basket on each arm. She may have to take off the pack, turn sideways, and decide where she can set each foot. Height gives you advantages wherever the world has room for them. The world is uneven in its generosity.

If people of different sizes use the same route regularly, you can see their decisions in the work they do on it. A landing wide enough for a Giant to turn with a load gives smaller travellers room to pass each other. Shorter steps along one edge can help someone who’d have to scramble up the taller ones. You can set a rail where a small person can reach it and give the taller person something to hold farther up. I like a path that gives people a choice about where they put their feet. It saves a great many discussions, particularly in a household where one person can change how tall she is and then insists she built everything to fit.

If you want a Giant’s opinion of a bridge, ask her to watch the bridge while somebody she trusts crosses first. She’ll learn something from the way it moves under a known load. She’ll also want to know what it’s built to carry. A bridge can be broad enough for her to walk across and still be unable to take her weight. The reverse is possible too. You can have a fine, strong crossing with an entrance she can barely get through. I have seen designs with both problems. The drawings were lovely. I’d have framed one if the person who drew it hadn’t needed the back to work out where the posts should go.

Good paths and bridges give Giants a way to bring what they carry into places that need it. Food can go up to a settlement whose lower road is closed. Timber can come down in lengths that would be awkward for smaller people to move by hand. A message can come with a person who’s seen the route and knows whether you ought to follow her back the way she came. None of those things is as dramatic as watching a Giant lift a boulder. I’d take the food and the news first, especially if the boulder is somewhere I’m perfectly happy to leave it.

The old war and the newer contest

The Giants and the Ogres went to war in the early world, before the elves came to know the Druids. I put that here because you’ll hear about the war when people talk about the Giants, and I’d rather you heard it alongside some of the other things they’ve done. An old war can have a very long life in a story. By the time a child hears it, the person telling it may have inherited a clear account of which pass was defended and a considerably less clear account of why anybody was fighting over it.

Some accounts of that war remember a pass, a journey, or the person who got a family out of danger more clearly than they remember who first made an accusation. I can tell you the war came before the Elves and Druids met. I cannot turn the uncertain parts of the accounts into an agreed cause simply because I am writing a chapter about Giants. You may keep the history in mind without asking every Giant you meet to carry responsibility for it.

Think about what it takes to move people and supplies through the mountains during a war. A weapon can help you hold a stretch of ground once you’ve reached it. Getting food there again tomorrow is a different piece of work. So is helping an injured person down a path you found difficult enough when everybody could walk. The same skills that make a Giant useful to her

neighbours in a hard winter can make her useful to the people she’s fighting beside. I’d keep that in mind when you hear somebody describe those skills as if they tell you what a Giant is inclined to do. Knowing how to carry a person downhill gives you a choice about what you do when somebody needs carrying.

That was the ancient war. The contest involving Minotaurs, Cyclopes, and a Giant belongs to a much later time, and to an arena rather than a battlefield. You’ve already heard me mention it. I was more interested in the disputed boundary than Selena thought appropriate. Here’s something else about the contest worth your attention. When people of very different sizes compete, a rule can look quite plain until somebody has to use it. Say the rule tells you to carry an object with both hands. A person with long arms may be able to bring her hands together around it and keep it close. Someone shorter may have to reach around the sides and carry it farther out. They’ve both followed the rule. You may want to think about what you meant to find out before you declare the result a surprise.

“Did you think about that before you suggested the boundary should be moved?” Selena asks.

“I suggested putting it somewhere everyone could agree on.”

“You suggested putting it where the Giant had already stopped.”

“She looked so pleased to be there.”

Selena has officiated at enough contests to know that looking pleased is a hopeless basis for a ruling. It remains a very good basis for buying somebody a drink afterwards.

What I’d ask you to notice

If you’re in a Giant’s company, watch what she does when she puts something down near you. Someone who can lower a load slowly enough for you to move your hand safely has a good deal of control. You may scarcely notice the effort if she does it well. That’s worth remembering when somebody wants to tell you strength is most impressive when you can hear it from the far side of a field. I’ve had a Giant put a cup down beside me without spilling a drop. The cup held enough tea for both Selena and me. It was a small cup to the woman who brought it, and she had remembered exactly how much room to leave for milk.

I think of the first people Selena and I made, and how often I’ve learnt about them by seeing what they do when they’re comfortable somewhere. A Giant on a path built for her can put her feet down where she means to and talk while she walks. A Giant eating with friends can set her weapon against the wall and ask who brought the bread. If nobody brought it, she can open a fourth pocket and find she had it all along. I like that moment very much. You can watch an entire table revise its opinion of the afternoon.

And if she offers to carry you up the mountain, you may say yes. Ask where she’d like your hands, tell her if you need to stop, and offer to carry something in return. She may give you the bread. Keep it where you can reach it. The view is better when you can both sit down and eat.

Glacies

If you’ve ever come in from the cold and leaned against a wall of ice, I expect you moved away from it. I did. The Glacies who’d offered me a seat asked whether I’d prefer another cushion. I said I’d prefer another wall. She put the cushion behind my back and went on pouring the tea. By the time I finished it, I’d begun to see what she meant.

You may be wondering why anyone would build a shelter out of the very thing they came inside to escape. The answer begins with the wind. Put a wall between yourself and a hard wind, and you can feel the change before you’ve got your other foot through the door. The wall can be cold enough to take the warmth out of your hand if you lean on it. It can still make the room much easier to warm by keeping the air you’ve already heated from being swept away. A cushion helps with the part where you lean on it. I mention this because I spent an embarrassing portion of my visit thinking the cushion was an apology for the wall.

The Glacies know what they’re doing with ice. Their homes can make you forget that there’s a difference between what they can live with and what they’ve learnt to build. I want to explain both. I also want to tell you why there are Glacies in the first place. Their palaces came later. It would have been a little forward of me to make somebody and expect her to spend the first afternoon deciding where she wanted the throne.

Why we made them

In the beginning there were places where winter stayed long enough to determine nearly everything about a visit. You could go up into the high country while the weather was clear, see a stretch of water that would be a lake again in summer, and come away with all sorts of ideas about the place. Most of those ideas would be about what you could do while you were there. What would you eat? Where could you sleep? How soon would you have to leave? Even a very attentive visitor has an eye on the journey home when she’s starting to lose the feeling in her feet.

I wanted there to be people who could stay and find out what happened after everyone else went home. What does a lake do halfway through a long freeze? Where does the meltwater go when the top of the slope warms and the bottom is still hard? Can you remember the shape of the ground well enough in winter to know where you’re standing when the snow covers the last thing you recognised? Those questions take time to answer. I could come back whenever I liked and still miss what changed between my visits. A person who lives there has the chance to see how one change leads to the next. She also has a reason to care whether she’ll be able to get down the same path tomorrow.

Selena asked me what I wanted those people to do once they could live there. I told her I thought they’d want to get to know the place.

“And after that?” she asked.

“I hope one of them will invite me in and tell me what she found.”

“You’ll have to knock.”

She says I leave that part out of the account because I find it inconvenient. I leave it out of a great many visits for precisely that reason. I am putting it here as evidence of my willingness to grow.

What I wanted for the Glacies was the freedom to make a life in cold that would have sent most of their neighbours down into the valleys. Their bodies needed to stay useful there. I mean useful in the intimate, daily sense. I wanted a person to be able to feel a fastening with her fingers, turn her head into the wind long enough to see who was calling, and finish a conversation outdoors if she liked the company. If she could do those things, she could decide whether she wanted to make a home, learn a trade, or spend the afternoon persuading somebody pretty to finish the conversation indoors. I have opinions about the last one. I’d have let her find her own way.

How a Glacies feels the cold

A Glacies can have a cool hand in a room where yours has warmed up. I noticed it when Queen Ilyss offered me hers. There was warmth in the places where her fingers pressed into mine, and coolness along the backs of them. Her hand moved easily. The tips of her fingers had all the feeling they needed to take a light hold. I mention this because people will sometimes feel a cool hand and immediately ask whether the person attached to it needs a fire. Ilyss needed me to let go once I’d finished greeting her. Even I eventually worked that out.

Think about what your fingers do when you’ve been outside too long on a cold day. You may still be able to grip a handle, but a little clasp can take three attempts. You find yourself looking at a thing you’d ordinarily do by touch. A Glacies can keep that fine feeling in conditions where you might already be putting your hands under your arms. The change in temperature is still information to her. She can feel where a rail has been out in the wind or where a warmer hand has rested on it. She has more time to use what she feels before the cold makes the task difficult. If you come across a Glacies dressed in layers, you can trust that she had her reasons. Walking into a headwind for an hour is different from crossing a courtyard. Standing still to speak to someone can cool you more than the walk that got you there. Clothing also keeps flying snow out of the collar and gives you something between your skin and the hard edge of whatever you’re carrying. A person can be well suited to the cold and have a favourite coat. I’m well suited to a great many conditions and have several coats I bought chiefly because Selena went quiet when I put them on.

The people nearest the door may be glad somebody has stopped opening it. The people near the fire may have been moving farther away by inches. A Glacies who’s been walking in cold air might want to undo a fastening before she sits. Give her a choice of seats. Ilyss had a beautiful place beside the window, and I was tempted to suppose she’d chosen it because the view was beautiful. She could see the whole court from

there, hear someone approaching along the passage, and get a little cooler air when she wanted it. She could also look very well in profile while doing all three. I give her credit for knowing that.

Why ice is worth building with

A Glacies who shapes ice has a few things to think about before she touches it. She needs to know how cold the place is likely to stay, where the weight will come down, and what will happen if the sun reaches one side longer than the other. Shaping an arch beautifully gets you as far as having a beautiful arch. If you’re hoping it will be there in the morning, you’ll want to think about where it meets the ground.

One way to understand the work is to look at a lamp behind clear ice. You can see the light because the ice lets it through. Where the surface is uneven, you may get several small bright edges where you expected one. Where the ice is clouded, the light spreads and the shape of the lamp becomes harder to see. Put those qualities to work in a building and you can make a passage that gets light from the court without having a window open to the wind. You can give somebody a private room with enough light to dress in. A person who knows her ice can choose which parts of the room she wants you to see from the passage, a consideration I have sometimes wished other builders would take more seriously.

There were arches above the frozen lake at Ilyss’s court. Lamps shone within them. You could see narrow stretches of clear ice lit from inside, with the stars still visible above. I like buildings that show you something about the material they were made from. Those arches could have been wood and held up the lamps very well. In ice, the light reached places there was no room to put a lamp. When you moved, you could see one bright line thin out and another take its place. I stood there longer than I meant to. A great many people could have accused me of watching the queen, and on this occasion they would only have been partly right.

Warmth has a place in that sort of work. Ice can be made to take a new shape where its surface is softened, then left to firm again in the cold. You can use a little water to join pieces, provided it freezes where you need the join and the pieces stay put while it does. I find the difficulty is usually getting the warmth to stop where you want it to stop. I can warm a particular place myself, and I’ve still had the experience of seeing a very small repair make its way slowly down the front of something I’d meant to keep. A Glacies who works with ice regularly knows what to look for before that happens. A line may turn wet farther from the join than she expected. She can feel a change in how a piece sits against its neighbour. If she sets a support in place before she starts, the rest of us may have no idea how much trouble she’s spared us.

You can see why the Glacies took an interest in ice once they had the means to live among it. If cold is going to stay for most of the year, a material that holds its shape in the cold is worth getting to know. The Glacies discovered where it would serve them well, where it needed help, and when they’d be happier with timber. I’m especially glad they came to that last conclusion. It gives me somewhere warm to put my hand when I’m admiring a room and have once again forgotten what the wall is made of.

The shelter on the pass

Ilyss invited me north over what turned out to be a roof. The White Horns had cleared the eastern pass. Then they’d taken the roof off the shelter beside it. The roof had been securely attached, which was one reason the Glacies were taking an interest in what the visitors intended to do next. You can make a very good case for asking a neighbour why she’s removed the roof. You can make an equally good case for asking her before offering a second building for her consideration. Ilyss was quite capable of doing both. She invited me because, as she put it, they believed the visitors were offering assistance and would like to discover with what.

The White Horns had brought timber. They’d brought blankets, too, which the Glacies took to be intended for them. Hessa explained that they were for travellers using the pass. There was food that turned out to have much the same purpose. The two groups could look at the same pile of supplies and have entirely different ideas about what work was already under way. It took drawing the shelter out on a low table and asking how somebody would use it to get the work in front of everybody at once.

Humans

I made humans to be able to learn anything. I probably should have told them that before they got so attached to doing things the way they’d done them the day before.

I mean anything in the sense that I never put a fence around what they could learn. A human could spend her childhood among people who’d never seen the sea and grow up to know more about tides than the people who sold her first boat. They’ve been doing a very good job of surprising me, although I’ve sometimes had to wait outside while they decided whether the door was supposed to be open. When I make a creature to live somewhere particular, I can begin with what the place will ask of her. I knew the Glacies would need to keep feeling in their fingers in cold weather. Her mother carried freight through the air. Istra found out she liked repairing steps. Every time I think of her, I’m glad the world had a loose stair tread in it at the right moment.

With humans, I wanted to leave more of the work of discovering what suited them to the humans themselves. She may not be able to tell you when she learnt to do it. She’ll notice if you give her a different tool, though. You can learn a great deal about the one you’ve handed her from the face she makes before she’s found a polite thing to say.

Humans can also learn to ask a different question from the one they began with. I find that rather more interesting. A woman may start with a problem about getting a cart over a stretch of bad ground. Someone shows her how a Goblin takes up a section of boardwalk and puts it down somewhere else. She thinks about what has to stay in place while the work is being done. The next time she looks at her cart, she wonders why she’s been trying to bring the whole thing across in one piece. The Goblin gave her something to think with. What she did with it afterwards belonged to her.

I’d hoped that would happen wherever humans met other peoples. It does, when they meet. I had a rather more generous idea of how often they’d arrange to do that.

The places they know

What a familiar life can hide

There’s a difference between what humans can learn and what they believe they’ll be able to learn. The second can settle very early. What it takes to shine

What they call Luk

A Leprechaun can look at you and see what could happen right now to make your life better or worse. They call it Luk. I’ve put it in italics because the last time I said it aloud, somebody asked whether I meant the sort you need to win at cards. You see how quickly a life can get worse. I’ve had no help at all.

The moment matters. You might be standing in a shop with your hand on a door and a question you’ve decided to take home with you. A Leprechaun looking at you could see that asking it now would do you some good. Come back tomorrow and the person you needed to ask might be gone. The Leprechaun can see what that turn is worth.

She can look at anybody. She needn’t know your name, share your ancestry, or have the slightest idea why you chose those trousers. “One could try.”

A different word might give her one last reason to stay home and tell herself the other person ought to come first. Either will sound reasonable to her, especially if it arrives when she’s looking for permission to do what’s easiest. That’s why you ought to pay attention to better for whom. You may be about to take a job that will make your employer very happy and leave you miserable. Your family may be delighted you’ve decided to stay where they can see you. You may want to go somewhere they’ve never heard of. Luk can show what will improve your life in the moment the Leprechaun is looking at you. What she does with that knowledge is her decision. There’s a great deal of trust in letting somebody see where one good word could do you the most good, especially when she can see where a bad one would hurt you most.

At its highest level, Luk can put an idea directly into your head. A master can leave you with several, and she can see which ideas belong together. One might give you a way to begin. Where the gold comes in

You’re going to ask me about gold. People usually do. Some are disappointed to hear that yellow light is quite easy to see on objects that are worth almost nothing. A word about lending light

I’ve seen a Leprechaun finish sewing by the light on her own hands, put the work down, and pull a blanket up to her chin because she’d had quite enough of everybody being able to see where she was. If somebody offers to share hers, I hope you’ll notice what she’s offering. It might be a few moments while you get a door open. It might be company all the way home. You can find out by asking. Why there’s one stem

When I explain why a Liliraune has one stem for two bodies, I start with the ground. A plant with roots in a place can make use of what’s there for a very long time. It can draw water from deeper down when the top layer begins to dry. It can spread into a patch of soil that stays cool a little longer than the rest. Give that same plant two living bodies above ground and the whole structure has to be able to supply both. The roots have more to support, and the stem has to carry what both bodies need without asking either one to stop living her own life while the other has her turn.

You can imagine solving the problem with two plants growing close together. Each would have its own roots and its own supply. They might shade each other. One might find water the other couldn’t reach. If the ground was good on only one side, you could have two neighbours a handspan apart living through quite different summers. A Liliraune takes another course. Both bodies grow from one living structure. If the roots find water, both have a claim on what comes up. A better place for one part of the root system can make a difference to both of them, even if only one has been sitting where she can see why the soil on that side stays wet.

What interests me about that arrangement is how much of it goes on while they’re doing something else. A Liliraune can be talking to you, thinking about lunch, or wondering whether the woman beside her is ever going to let her finish a sentence. The water still has to move through the stem. The roots still have to hold the flower where it stands. You have work going on in your own body that you scarcely attend to until it gives you trouble. The capacity to be Wiccan belongs to those who can feel the entrance of magic, whatever people they come from. If you’ve been waiting to see the right kind of person walk through your door before you ask whether she can do either, you may have kept a very good pupil waiting outside. Invite her in. Ask her what she came to learn. If she brought a bowl, find out what she’d like to put in it.

What we can take home

Breni’s looking at the table again. The duck’s been picked over, the venison has gone cold, and there are enough potatoes left to make a convincing case for a second supper.

“We could take some of it with us,” Reyla says.

“I’ve been trying to work out how much of it counts as some,” Breni tells her.

“The bread and the duck?”

“Then there’s the venison.”

“Take that too.”

“And the potatoes?”

Reyla considers it. “I think we’ve gone round the table and agreed to take everything.” Selena lifts the empty plates into a stack without touching them. The clean cloth she brought folds itself around the bread. I’m watching her tie the knot from across the table when I feel that little warmth again. It’s moved to the place beneath my ear where she likes to kiss me. She has the nerve to ask how much more I have to write.

“You could find out by letting me finish,” I tell her.

“I could.”

She’s made no attempt to do so. I married her with a very good understanding of this quality in her, and I find it extremely inconvenient when I’m trying to prove I have some of my own. You’ve heard me spend a great many pages telling you what people were given and what they did with it. We’ve had a chance to see what happens when somebody takes a gift farther than I thought to take it myself. I wanted people to be able to make their lives here. Some of them have shown me parts of this world I’d never have understood from making it. A witch is a good person to end with. She learns something that can change the way her day goes, then she may spend years finding out what else she can do with it. You can leave her with a warm bowl in the

morning and come back in the evening to find she’s thought of a way to get supper to somebody who needed it. I like the prospect of coming back and being told how she managed it.

I’ll have to leave the next person to tell me that story when I see her. Selena and I should return to the new reality. It’s still new enough to need our attention. There are things we put there that have barely had time to find out what they’re going to do, and I’d like to be around for some of the answers. I also know myself well enough to say that if I wait until this table is cleared, somebody will ask whether we want another pot of tea and I’ll still be here when breakfast comes.

“Is that the only reason you want to leave now?” Selena asks.

“No, darling. You know the other one perfectly well.”

The warmth at my neck finally stops. She offers me her actual hand. I like that even better.

Before we go

Reyla, I want to tell you something before I close the book. When I offered you the form you’re sitting in, you chose to take it so we could have you here with us in a way we could see and touch. You’ve had time to talk to all three of us. Breni has had time to hold you. I’ve had time to remember how much I like looking across a table and seeing you there. The form is yours now. You can call on it whenever you see fit. You can return to your life with the tree whenever you want, and you can come and sit beside somebody when you want that, too. You won’t have to wait for me to ask.

“Even if I only want to come back for the end of a conversation?” Reyla asks.

“Especially then. You usually ask the question I’ve been hoping nobody will think of.”

“Can I use it wherever I choose?”

“Yes. You’re still part of the world. This is another way for you to be with the people in it.”

Reyla looks at Breni. “Would you mind if I stayed a little longer tonight?”

“I was hoping you would,” Breni says. “We’ve got all these potatoes.”

Reyla laughs and takes her hand. I’d been thinking about telling her how it would work in more detail. She’s already deciding where she wants to be. She’s understood everything she needs to understand.

Selena opens the way to the new reality beside us. I can feel the air from the other side, and there’s a smell I haven’t had enough time there to name. I’ll have to find out what it belongs to. It can wait another moment. Breni has got up from the table.

“Thank you, Plu,” she says.

“You’re welcome.”

She kisses me before I can say anything else. A warm, unhurried kiss. She’s very good at knowing when I’ve said enough, though I don’t think she’s ever tried quite so agreeable a way of proving it. When she lets me go, Reyla’s still holding her hand. Selena’s waiting for mine. I take it. You can still see the courtyard from here. There are cups on the table and a loaf wrapped up to go home with two women who’ve decided to spend a little longer together. Beyond Selena, there’s a place that’s only just beginning to find out what it can be. She pulls me gently towards it. As we disappear, I turn my head and whisper to you.

“They have no idea what they’re in for, but it’s gonna be a blast.”
